## 1. BTK — The Floppy Disk That Turned In Its Owner

On January 15th, 1974, in Wichita, Kansas, 4 members of one family were murdered inside their own home. It was the beginning of a killing spree that ran 17 years and claimed at least 10 victims, ending with a final murder in January of 1991.

The killer liked to write letters. He sent them to newspapers and to the police, describing in detail what he had done, and he gave himself a nickname. BTK. 3 letters standing for bind, torture, and kill. He chose that name for himself, and the Wichita press used it for decades.

Then in 1979, the letters stopped. BTK went quiet. For the next 25 years there were no letters and no new murders. The city slowly came to believe he had died or moved away.

In January of 2004, the Wichita Eagle ran a piece marking 30 years since that first unsolved murder. The man read it.

When he was finally caught, he explained why he started writing again, and the reason was chilling in how ordinary it was. He said he was bored. In March of 2004, after a quarter century of silence, BTK started mailing letters again.

Somewhere in that exchange, he asked the police a question of his own. If he sent them information on a floppy disk, could they trace it back to him?

The police answered by running a coded message in the newspaper telling him it would be safe.

That was a lie.

On February 16th, 2005, a purple one-point-four-four megabyte floppy disk arrived at a Wichita television station, containing a letter, a necklace, and a book cover.

Technicians recovered a deleted Word file from that disk. What was on it wasn't a murder plan. It was the agenda for a church council meeting.

But every Word file carries a hidden layer of information the user never sees on screen, recording who created it and who edited it last. On this file, that layer held two things. The name of a church, Christ Lutheran Church. And the person who last saved it, listed as a single word. Dennis.

Police pulled up the church's website and found the name of its council president. Dennis Rader.

Dennis Rader, born in 1945, had a wife and two children. He had spent 14 years installing home security alarm systems, which meant he was paid to walk into other people's houses and study how those houses were protected. After that he worked as a dogcatcher and compliance officer for his town. He was an adult Boy Scout leader. And in January of 2005, one month before he mailed that floppy disk, he had just been elected president of his church council.

To be certain, investigators got a court order for an old medical sample belonging to Rader's daughter, taken at the clinic of the university she had attended. Her DNA showed a direct blood relationship to DNA recovered from under a victim's fingernails in 1986.

Rader was arrested on February 25th, 2005, 9 days after mailing the disk. On August 18th, 2005, he received 10 consecutive life sentences with a minimum of 175 years before any parole consideration.

For 31 years, nothing the police did ever touched him. What finally caught him was a question he asked himself, and an answer he chose to believe.

## 2. John List — A Face Sculpted in Clay 18 Years Later

On November 9th, 1971, in Westfield, New Jersey, a 46 year old accountant named John List murdered his entire family.

He shot his wife Helen, 47, in the back of the head while the children were still at school. Then he went up to the attic and shot his own mother Alma, 84. After that he made himself lunch and sat down to eat it.

As the children came home one by one, he shot them one by one. Patricia, 16. Frederick, 13. The last one home was John Junior, 15, back from soccer practice. He was shot multiple times, and the evidence at the scene showed he had tried to fight back.

Their home was a 19 room Victorian mansion with its own ballroom. List laid sleeping bags out on that ballroom floor and arranged the bodies of his wife and 3 children on top of them, neatly. He left his mother's body where it was in the attic.

The reason, according to testimony at his trial years later, started with money. In 1971 the bank where he worked shut down and he lost his job. He told no one in the house. Every morning he still put on a suit, picked up his briefcase, left on schedule, and sat somewhere until the day was over. He drained his mother's accounts to keep up the mortgage payments, and pushed his children to find part time work.

He wrote a 5 page letter to his pastor explaining that there was too much evil in the world, and that by killing his family while they were still innocent he was saving their souls.

Then he did a series of very calm things. He drove to the bank and closed every account. He stopped by his son's soccer game so everything would look normal. He turned the heat in the house down low to slow decomposition. He tuned a radio to a religious station and left it playing. He left notes for the school and the neighbors saying the family had gone to visit their sick grandmother in North Carolina and would be away for weeks.

And he cut his own face out of every family photograph in the house.

Nobody noticed anything for almost a month. The Lists were a private family who kept to themselves. The one odd thing the neighbors could see was that every light in the mansion burned day and night, with nobody ever moving inside.

Then the bulbs began to burn out, one at a time. As the house went dark, the neighbors finally called the police. On December 7th, 1971, officers climbed through an unlocked basement window and went in.

John List had been gone almost a month by then. He took a train to Michigan, then to Colorado, and by early 1972 he had settled in Denver under a new name. Robert Clark. He took it from an old college classmate, a man who later said he had never known List at all.

He lived that way for 17 years and 6 months. He worked as an accountant, joined a Lutheran congregation, and in 1985 he got married. His new wife had no idea who she had married.

In 1989, a new Fox television program called America's Most Wanted decided to feature the case. Their problem was obvious. The only photograph of John List was 18 years old, and nobody knew what he looked like now.

They went to a forensic sculptor named Frank Bender. He built a clay bust, not of List's face in 1971, but of what that face would look like after 18 years of aging, accounting for hair loss, sagging, and even the style of glasses frames a man with that personality would most likely choose.

The bust aired on May 21st, 1989.

In Colorado, a woman named Wanda Flanery was watching with her daughter. Both of them looked at that clay head and thought of the same person, their old neighbor Robert Clark. They called the show.

On June 1st, 1989, less than 2 weeks after the broadcast, police walked into the accounting office where he worked in Virginia and arrested him.

He insisted he was Robert Clark for months. Only when they confronted him with fingerprints from his own military service record did he admit his real name, on February 16th, 1990.

On April 12th, 1990, John List was convicted on 5 counts of first degree murder. The judge said from the bench that he was a man without remorse and without honor. He was given 5 consecutive life sentences, leaving him ineligible for parole for nearly 125 years.

He died in prison in 2008 at 82 years old. The clay bust that caught him is still on display in a crime museum.

## 3. Colonel Russell Williams — Tire Tracks in the Snow

In January of 2010, in Ontario, Canada, a 27 year old woman named Jessica Lloyd vanished from her home. 2 months earlier, an air force corporal named Marie-France Comeau had been found dead in hers.

Ontario Provincial Police went to Jessica Lloyd's house and found two things in the snow behind it. Tire tracks from a vehicle, and a man's boot prints.

That was everything they had.

On the evening of Thursday, February 4th, 2010, police set up a roadblock on Highway 37, the route connecting 2 nearby towns. They stopped every vehicle that came through and did one simple thing. They looked at the tires.

One of the vehicles that pulled up to that roadblock was a Nissan Pathfinder. The driver was Colonel Russell Williams, commander of Canadian Forces Base Trenton, the largest military airbase in Canada, a pilot who had flown Queen Elizabeth II and the Prime Minister of Canada. Marie-France Comeau, the woman found dead 2 months earlier, had been a servicewoman under his own command.

That night, by pure chance, he was driving the Pathfinder instead of the BMW he normally drove.

The officers at the roadblock looked down at his tires and saw a tread pattern matching the tracks in the snow behind Jessica Lloyd's house.

On February 7th, Williams was invited to Ottawa police headquarters to assist with the investigation. He drove himself there, alone, with no lawyer. He wore the same boots he had worn the night Jessica Lloyd disappeared.

The interview began at 3 in the afternoon, conducted by Detective Sergeant Jim Smyth. It was recorded in full, and it later became one of the most studied interrogation tapes in police work.

Smyth never raised his voice. He simply laid the evidence on the table one piece at a time. The tire tracks match your vehicle. The distance between the wheels matches your vehicle. A witness saw a vehicle like yours behind her house. And the boots you are wearing right now match the prints in the snow.

At 7:45 that evening, 4 hours and 45 minutes in, Colonel Russell Williams started talking.

He didn't just confess to 2 murders. He described 82 break ins over 3 years, mostly to steal women's and girls' underwear, along with multiple sexual assaults. He told police exactly where every stolen item was hidden inside his own home in Ottawa. And he drew them a map to where he had left Jessica Lloyd's body.

On October 18th, 2010, Williams pleaded guilty to everything. 4 days later he received 2 life sentences with no parole eligibility for 25 years.

The Canadian military stripped him of his rank and burned the uniform he had worn.

## 4. The Unabomber — The Woman Who Recognized Her Brother-in-Law's Voice

From 1978 to 1995, for 17 years, someone mailed bombs to universities, airlines, and technology companies across the United States. 16 devices in total. 3 people killed, 23 injured.

The FBI named the case UNABOM, combining "university" and "airline", the first two categories of target. The press called him the Unabomber.

The investigation ran nearly two decades and cost over $50 million, making it the most expensive FBI investigation up to that point. They had bomb fragments. They had a signature level of craftsmanship. They even had a sketch of a hooded suspect in sunglasses that the entire country could recognize on sight.

What they didn't have was a name.

In 1995, the bomber sent newspapers a manuscript running about 35,000 words, titled Industrial Society and Its Future, a long indictment of modern technology. It came with a condition. If a major newspaper printed it in full, he would stop bombing.

Printing it meant giving a terrorist exactly what he wanted. Not printing it might mean more people dead.

The Justice Department and the FBI decided to print it, not because they trusted his promise, but because of a different calculation. 35,000 words is a lot of words. If the whole country read it, maybe somebody would recognize how the man wrote.

On September 19th, 1995, the Washington Post published the entire manuscript.

The person who recognized it wasn't an investigator at all.

Linda Patrik was a philosophy professor living in New York State. Her husband, David Kaczynski, had an older brother named Ted, a strange man who had walked away from everything to live alone in the woods, and with whom the family had almost no contact.

Linda's suspicion actually started before the manuscript ever ran. In 1995, while she and David were in Paris during a wave of bombings in that city, she had a strong intuition about her brother-in-law. She said so out loud to her husband. David dismissed it.

Then the manuscript appeared in the Washington Post, and Linda read arguments that felt sickeningly familiar. They matched what she had read in letters Ted had sent David through the 1970s, including a 23 page essay attacking the modern world in the exact same voice.

She handed the manuscript to her husband and told him to read it.

David didn't want to. Because if she was right, it meant his own brother had killed 3 people, and he would be the one who had to turn him in.

Linda argued him around using an idea from Buddhism. If Ted really was the man, then stopping him was good for the victims and good for Ted himself, because it ended the harm still being done. She took David to the library, and the two of them sat and read the manuscript together.

David read it, and he heard his brother. Not any single sentence, but the shape of the reasoning, the word choices, the particular phrases. He dug through old family papers and found essays Ted had written years earlier using the same phrases and the same arguments.

Ted Kaczynski had been a mathematical prodigy. An IQ of 167. Accepted to Harvard at 16. A doctorate in mathematics from the University of Michigan, with a dissertation that won the prize for the best math dissertation that year. At 25 he became the youngest assistant professor in the history of the University of California at Berkeley. 2 years later he abruptly resigned with no explanation and moved into a remote wooden cabin in Montana with no electricity and no running water.

David didn't go straight to the FBI. He hired a private investigator in Chicago, then a lawyer in Washington. The lawyer brought in a criminal profiler to compare the writings, and the answer came back at better than 60 percent odds of the same author. In February of 1996, the lawyer finally handed the FBI an essay Ted had written back in 1971 for comparison.

On April 3rd, 1996, agents moved in on the cabin in Montana. Inside they found bomb components, one fully assembled bomb ready to mail, homemade firearms, the original manuscript of the manifesto, and roughly 40,000 handwritten journal pages meticulously documenting every experiment and every bombing.

Ted Kaczynski pleaded guilty in 1998 and received multiple life sentences without parole. He was not executed.

David Kaczynski spent years afterward campaigning against the death penalty. The brother he turned in never forgave him. Ted Kaczynski killed himself in prison on June 10th, 2023, after a cancer diagnosis and after stopping treatment.

## 5. The Golden State Killer — A Family Tree of Strangers

Between 1974 and 1986, one man committed one of the longest crime sprees in California history. More than 220 burglaries, at least 51 rapes, and 13 murders, spread from the north of the state to the south.

Because the crimes happened in different regions years apart, police originally thought they were dealing with different men and gave him different nicknames. It wasn't until 2001 that DNA testing proved they were all the same person. Years later a crime writer gave him the name that finally stuck. The Golden State Killer.

After 1986 he simply stopped. Nothing more.

For over 3 decades police had his DNA and nothing to match it against. DNA is only useful when the suspect's own sample already sits in a database, and he had never been arrested for anything, so he wasn't in any database at all.

In December of 2017, a team of investigators tried something that had never been used to solve a case before. They didn't look for the killer. They looked for his relatives.

By then, millions of Americans were mailing saliva samples to commercial DNA services to learn about their ancestry, and many uploaded their results to public genealogy sites to find lost relatives. The team uploaded the killer's DNA profile, taken from a rape kit, to one of those sites, called GEDmatch.

What came back were 10 to 20 people who shared a set of great great great grandparents with the killer. Completely innocent people, most of whom had never met each other, connected only by one couple from the 19th century.

Then came the hard part. Working with a genealogist named Barbara Rae-Venter, the team built roughly 25 family trees, one of them containing close to 1,000 people. They traced downward from those distant relatives through every branch of descendants, eliminating people by age, by sex, and by where they had lived when the crimes happened.

By April of 2018, one name on that enormous tree could not be eliminated. Joseph James DeAngelo, 72 years old.

A family tree isn't enough to arrest anyone. They needed DeAngelo's own DNA.

On April 18th, 2018, investigators quietly collected a sample from the door handle of his car. Then they took a second sample from a tissue in the garbage he had rolled out to the curb.

Both matched the DNA from the crime scenes.

DeAngelo was arrested on April 24th, 2018, 32 years after anyone had come close to him.

And his own record reads unlike anything else in this story. Joseph DeAngelo had been a police officer. He earned a criminal justice degree in 1971, joined the force in 1973, and worked in a burglary investigation unit during the exact period he was committing his own string of burglaries and rapes. He was fired in 1979 for something completely unrelated. He was caught shoplifting from a grocery store.

On June 29th, 2020, DeAngelo pleaded guilty to 13 murders and 13 kidnappings in exchange for prosecutors dropping the death penalty. On August 21st, 2020, he received 13 consecutive life sentences without parole.

In court, after listening to dozens of surviving victims and family members of the dead speak one after another, he stood and said one short sentence. That he had listened to every one of their statements, and that he was truly sorry to everyone he had hurt.

The genetic genealogy method used in this case has since been applied to hundreds of other cold cases across the country.

## 6. Gilgo Beach — A Pizza Crust in a Trash Can

On the night of May 1st, 2010, a 24 year old woman named Shannan Gilbert called 911 from inside a house in Oak Beach, on Long Island, New York. She said someone was after her. She ran out into the dark, and no one ever saw her again.

Police searched along the coastal road nearby. Between December 11th and 13th of 2010, while looking for Shannan Gilbert, they found the bodies of 4 other women, wrapped in burlap, scattered through the brush along the roadside.

In March and April of 2011, they found 6 more sets of remains.

Shannan Gilbert's body, the woman the search had been for in the first place, wasn't found until December of 2011, in a marsh, 19 months after she disappeared.

Then the case froze for more than a decade. No suspect. No movement.

In 2022, an inter agency task force was formed to reopen the entire file.

The thread they pulled came from a witness statement more than 10 years old, describing a first generation Chevrolet Avalanche pickup, dark green, connected to one of the victims. The task force ran regional vehicle registration data and found a man who owned exactly that truck. Rex Heuermann, an architect with an office in Manhattan, living with his wife and children on Long Island.

The calls connected to 3 of the first 4 victims had all been made from burner phones, bought with cash, registered to nobody. But every time one of those phones connected, it still logged the nearest cell tower. When investigators lined that chain of towers up against Heuermann's own movements, they matched.

It still wasn't enough. They needed DNA.

Investigators followed Heuermann for months. In January of 2023, he ate lunch near his Manhattan office and threw a pizza box into a public trash can. Investigators retrieved the box and the crust he had left behind.

The DNA from that pizza crust matched DNA recovered from a hair found on the burlap wrapped around one of the victims.

Rex Heuermann was arrested on July 13th, 2023.

He was initially charged with 3 counts of murder. Over the next 2 years, as more evidence was connected, the charges grew, and the timeline of the killings was pushed back as far as 1993, a full decade earlier than police had ever believed.

On April 8th, 2026, Rex Heuermann pleaded guilty to 7 murders. In court, he also admitted to an 8th that he had never been charged with. On June 17th, 2026, less than 2 months ago, he was sentenced to life in prison without parole.

But there is still one name missing from that sentence. Shannan Gilbert, the woman who called 911 in the night and ran into the dark, the woman whose search led to the discovery of every other victim, was never connected to Rex Heuermann. The cause of her death is still listed in the official record as a single word. Undetermined.
