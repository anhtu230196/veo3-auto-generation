# Do Animals Hold Grudges? (And Do They Actually Grieve?) — English narration (approved 2026-07-31, ElevenLabs-friendly punctuation)

In Seattle, there's a man that the crows on the University of Washington campus have despised for over a decade, even though he never did anything to them personally. The story starts back in 2006. To tag crows for a research study, lab assistants had to wear a rubber caveman mask every time they went out to trap birds, so the crows wouldn't memorize their actual faces and hold it against them. After a few trapping sessions the team figured that would be the end of it. They were badly mistaken. Years later, anyone who so much as walked across campus wearing that exact mask got mobbed on sight, including people who had never trapped a single crow in their life. They got dive-bombed, scolded, and tailed from a distance. Even stranger, crows born long after the original trapping still recognized the mask on sight, almost like someone had personally sat them down and explained that this face meant trouble.

We tend to assume that holding onto a grudge across generations is a distinctly human habit, the kind of thing grandparents pass down at the dinner table. But if I told you there are crows out there who've never met you personally and are still teaching their kids to hate one specific rubber face, you might start feeling a lot more forgiving toward your difficult neighbor.

The crows' long memory doesn't stop at petty revenge though. It leads somewhere much stranger.

A different researcher at that same university named Kaeli Swift ran an experiment where she placed the body of a crow that had died of natural causes in an area wild crows regularly visited to feed. Within minutes the crows started gathering, more and more of them, circling and cawing loudly, not eating and not doing much of anything except staring and calling out. Here's the part that really got people's attention. For days afterward that same group of crows avoided the exact spot where the body had been, even with plenty of food still sitting right there. One crow's death wasn't just a passing disturbance. It became a lesson the whole group remembered and apparently passed along, something like a warning that something bad had happened there and it was best to steer clear.

Sound familiar? It's basically an entire neighborhood quietly agreeing to avoid the house where something sad happened, no matter how good the bakery next door is.

Now let's leave the sky and head down to the African savanna, where scientists once ran an experiment that sounds a little morbid but turns out to be genuinely fascinating. They laid out three objects in front of a group of elephants: a plain block of wood, a buffalo skull, and an elephant skull. The elephants barely glanced at the wood or the buffalo skull, but they lingered for a long time around the elephant skull, gently running their trunks over it, tracing the curve of it, lifting a piece of tusk and setting it back down, almost like flipping through an old photo album. For a species with no hands to turn pages, the trunk is really the only way to read something that still feels familiar.

If you think elephants and crows are just isolated cases, head out to sea for a moment to the waters off Seattle, home to a bit of a celebrity named Tahlequah, a female orca also known by her ID, J35.

In 2018, right after Tahlequah gave birth to a calf that survived less than half an hour, the whole world watched, practically holding its breath, as she used her head to push her calf's body along the surface of the water. She swam with it for 17 straight days, over 1,600 kilometers, diving down to lift the body back up every single time it slipped under. What seemed like a once in a lifetime story ended up happening again. In early January 2025 Tahlequah lost another calf, and researchers at the Center for Whale Research confirmed she was once again carrying the body on her rostrum, for at least 11 straight days, the exact same thing she had done seven years earlier.

So there it is. Crows that remember an enemy's face across generations and hold something like a formal gathering around a body. Elephants that trace over a skull of their own kind like flipping through a memory. A mother orca who twice now has refused to simply let go. Three completely different brains, all reacting in a way that leaves the people watching a little stunned.

At this point someone's bound to push back and ask if we're reading way too much into this. Maybe the crows are just learning a straightforward danger signal, maybe the elephants are just curious about a novel object, and maybe the orca simply hasn't caught up to the fact that her calf is gone. It's a completely fair question, fair enough that when primatologist Barbara King wrote an entire book on the subject she laid out a careful checklist before she would call any behavior genuine grief. There has to be evidence the two animals were actually bonded, and the surviving animal has to show a clear sustained change in behavior afterward, refusing to eat, pulling away from the group, or lingering in one place far longer than usual. Tahlequah abandoned her normal feeding pattern for nearly three weeks straight. That group of crows rerouted their usual flight paths for days. That's not a split second reflex that fades in an instant. It's a prolonged disruption, exactly the kind of thing we'd expect to see when someone is genuinely and deeply grieving.

And here's the part I find most worth sitting with. Crows, elephants, and marine mammals like orcas split onto separate evolutionary paths ages ago, with no shared ancestor anywhere close enough to have known what sadness even was. And yet all three, evolving in complete isolation from one another, arrived at a strikingly similar response to the exact same trigger, the permanent disappearance of someone they were bonded to. Maybe the capacity for grief isn't some advanced feature that only a human level brain is sophisticated enough to run. It looks more like an unavoidable cost that comes bundled with real social bonding wherever it happens to exist. Anywhere something learns to care, it eventually learns to hurt when that gets taken away.

For humans the oldest trace of this sits right under the ground. Neanderthal remains have been found not scattered haphazardly like abandoned bodies, but carefully arranged, curled into deliberately dug pits, the earliest known evidence of burial. There are even more contested finds, like the burial-like markers attributed to Homo naledi from roughly 300,000 years ago, though scientists are still far from settled on that dating. But if it holds up, it turns out humans didn't invent the funeral. We just showed up later and started handling the invitations.

Let's head back to that University of Washington campus one last time. The research assistant who wore that mask all those years ago left the university long ago. But the crows there, generation after generation, still haven't forgotten. Somebody really ought to warn the incoming freshmen that if a crow ever dive-bombs them for no obvious reason, they shouldn't assume it's just bad luck. They might simply have inherited a grudge older than they are.

So if your dog or cat suddenly goes quiet, stops eating, and keeps returning to the same spot a departed animal friend used to occupy, don't be too quick to write it off as you projecting feelings onto them. Somewhere out there a crow is teaching its kid to remember a face. An elephant is pausing beside a skull that feels familiar. And out past the horizon, Tahlequah is still swimming on with her life, carrying a memory that doesn't need a single word to make itself understood.
