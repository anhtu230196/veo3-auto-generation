## 1. The Flying Tailor

On February 4th, 1912, Franz Reichelt arrived at the Eiffel Tower at seven in the morning in the freezing Paris cold. He was already wearing the suit of fabric he had sewn himself.

Reichelt was born in Austria in 1878. He moved to Paris and built a successful tailoring business. He read about pilots dying in falls from airplanes and became fixated on sewing a suit that could double as a parachute, something a pilot could wear to fly and then open up if he fell.

His early tests looked promising. He dropped dummies wearing the suit from the fifth floor of his apartment building. But he could never repeat that result in his later designs. In 1911 he tried the suit himself, jumping from a window about 26 feet up, and broke his leg. Instead of doubting the design, Reichelt decided the problem was the height. The parachute needed more time in the air to open all the way. He just needed a taller drop.

He petitioned the Paris police department again and again for permission to test the suit from the Eiffel Tower. Early in 1912 they finally agreed, on one condition. Whoever jumped had to be a dummy. Not him.

For more than an hour, as the crowd below kept growing, Reichelt told the onlookers, "I want to try the experiment myself, without any trickery, because I'm the one who can prove this invention actually works."

Then he started climbing the stairs to the first platform. Partway up, he stopped, turned around, waved to the crowd, and called out "See you soon" in French.

At the platform, about 187 feet up, he stepped onto the railing. Then he stepped off.

The suit folded around him almost instantly. He dropped straight down onto the frozen ground. The newspaper Le Petit Parisien reported his right leg and arm were crushed. His skull and spine were broken. He died on the spot.

A film crew had captured the entire fall.

## 2. The Submarine That Wouldn't Surface

On October 15th, 1863, in Charleston harbor, Horace Lawson Hunley, thirty nine years old, climbed down into the hatch of the submarine that carried his name to personally command a training run.

Hunley was an engineer and businessman who had paid out of his own pocket to build the Confederacy's first military submarine. The vessel was about 39 feet long, built out of an old steam boiler, with room for one pilot and seven men to hand crank the propeller. The sub's regular captain, Lieutenant George Dixon, was away that day. So Hunley took the wheel himself.

The exercise was to dive under an anchored ship, the CSS Indian Chief, and surface on the other side. That was the exact move they planned to use to sneak up on a real enemy vessel. A little over a month earlier, this same submarine had already sunk once. A crewman had stepped on the diving plane lever by accident while a hatch was still open, drowning five men right there in the harbor.

This time, the sub went under and never came back up.

Bad weather kept divers away for weeks. On November 7th, they finally located it. The bow was buried deep in the harbor mud. The stern was still floating. When they pried open the hatches, they found the forward ballast valve wide open. The wrench used to close it was lying loose on the floor. Thomas Park was found at the aft hatch. Hunley was at the forward hatch, still gripping a candle that had long since gone out.

All eight men aboard were dead. Hunley and Park had suffocated at the two hatches. The other six had drowned in the flooded hull.

On November 8th, Hunley was buried with full military honors at Magnolia Cemetery in Charleston. The next day, the other seven crewmen were buried beside him, under a volley of gunfire and the sound of taps.

Four months later, that same submarine, with a third crew aboard, became the first in history to sink an enemy warship in combat. That same night, it went down again. It took all eight men of that third crew with it.

## 3. Teacher of the Birds

On August 9th, 1896, Otto Lilienthal, forty eight years old, carried his homemade glider up the Rhinow hills near Stölln, northwest of Berlin, to fly like it was any ordinary weekend.

Lilienthal was a German engineer, the first person to prove a human being could glide under control on rigid wings, after more than 2,000 test flights over the years. He was also the first person to manufacture and sell gliders to anyone who wanted to learn to fly. The book he wrote on how birds fly would go on to directly inspire the Wright brothers.

The day before, he had written to invite Robert Wood, an American chemistry student in Berlin, to come watch him fly. Wood turned him down, too busy with other work. The next morning Lilienthal went out to the hills with a few helpers and pulled off several short flights without any trouble.

Then came his fourth flight of the day. Everything changed.

He had just left the slope when the glider suddenly pitched its nose up. He tried rocking his body backward to level it out, but it had already lost all its lift. It dropped almost straight down from about 50 feet. The fall snapped his third cervical vertebra. He was taken to a doctor nearby, then put on a train to Berlin, to the clinic of the surgeon Ernst von Bergmann.

About 36 hours after the fall, on August 10th, Lilienthal died without ever fully regaining consciousness.

More than thirty years later, his sister in law said his last words were, "Sacrifices must be made." Many historians doubt that's true. The line was later carved into his gravestone.

## 4. The Train Car With Wings

On July 24th, 1921, Valerian Abakovsky, twenty five years old, boarded the high speed railcar he had designed himself to carry a delegation of international officials from Moscow to the coal mines at Tula.

Abakovsky was a Latvian born engineer who had worked as a driver for the Cheka secret police before he was put in charge of designing this machine. His railcar, called the Aerowagon, had an airplane engine and propeller bolted onto the front. It could hit close to 90 miles an hour, far faster than any ordinary train of the time. It had originally been built to carry urgent government papers and officials. The passengers that day were delegates to the first congress of the Profintern, the international communist trade union body. It was led by the Bolshevik politician Fyodor Sergeyev, along with union delegates from Germany, Britain, Australia, and Bulgaria. They were headed out to tour the coal mines and a weapons factory.

The trip out to Tula, running at about 25 miles an hour, went off without a hitch.

On the way back, the delegation pushed to go faster to make good time. The railcar's speed climbed to around 50 miles an hour.

The tracks could not handle that.

At 6:35 that evening, about 70 miles outside Moscow, near the town of Serpukhov, the railcar jumped the tracks and tore itself apart. The official investigation blamed worn out rails. Years later, Sergeyev's son said investigators had found a pile of rocks deliberately placed on the tracks at the exact spot of the derailment. He suspected sabotage aimed at his father. The claim was never confirmed.

Seven of the twenty two people aboard died. Fyodor Sergeyev. Valerian Abakovsky. Two German delegates, Otto Strupat and Oskar Hellbrück. British delegate John William Hewlett. Bulgarian delegate Ivan Konstantinov. And Australian delegate Paul Freeman. He died of his injuries a short time later.

The seven bodies lay in state at the House of Unions in Moscow, then were buried together in the Kremlin Wall. It was the last mass burial ever held on the grounds of Red Square. Sergeyev's son was barely four months old and now fatherless. Lenin personally arranged for Stalin to adopt him. The boy grew up in Stalin's household and later became a major general in the Red Army.

## 5. First to Fly, First to Fall

On June 15th, 1785, Jean-François Pilâtre de Rozier, thirty one years old, took off from Boulogne-sur-Mer to fly a hot air balloon across the English Channel, from France to England.

De Rozier was the son of an innkeeper from Metz. He had dropped out of training as a military surgeon because he couldn't stand dissections, and turned to chemistry and physics instead. On November 21st, 1783, he and the Marquis d'Arlandes became the first two human beings in history to fly free in a balloon. It was a 25 minute flight that reached about 3,000 feet and covered roughly six miles over the skies of Paris. He flew twice more in 1784. But crossing the Channel was still unfinished business. Five months earlier, two other men had crossed it by balloon, flying from England to France. Nobody had made the crossing the other way yet.

The winds over the Channel wouldn't carry an ordinary hot air balloon far enough. To fix that, de Rozier designed a hybrid balloon nobody had ever tried before. A hydrogen balloon on top for extra lift. A hot air balloon burning on an open flame right underneath it to control altitude.

He put an open flame directly beneath the most flammable gas known at the time.

About 27 minutes into the flight, at roughly 1,700 feet, a gust of wind carried a spark up from the stove into the balloon above. It burned a hole through the thin fabric and ignited the hydrogen inside. Fire swallowed both balloons almost instantly. A letter written at the time from Boulogne described the scene. "The two adventurers were dashed to pieces as they fell." De Rozier and his companion Pierre Romain, 27 years old, dropped straight down, landing about a thousand feet from the water's edge. De Rozier died the moment he hit the ground. Romain was still conscious. He asked for water, then died shortly after.

They became the first two people in history to die in an aviation accident. It hadn't even been two years since de Rozier himself became the first person ever to fly.

## 6. The Man in Front of the Flame

On May 17th, 1930, at the Heylandt Works in Berlin, Max Valier, thirty five years old, sat down in front of a rocket combustion chamber for the third test run of the night.

Valier was Austrian, fascinated by astronomy since childhood. He studied physics in Innsbruck and got swept up in Hermann Oberth's ideas about space travel. He wrote a popular science book on rocketry that went through six printings, and in 1927 helped found the Verein für Raumschiffahrt, the largest amateur rocket society in the world at the time. Its members would later include Wernher von Braun. Working with the industrialist Fritz von Opel, he built the world's first rocket powered car in 1928, which topped 140 miles an hour, followed by a rocket plane and a rocket sled. Just a month before that night, on April 19th, 1930, he had successfully test driven the first car ever powered by liquid rocket fuel, the Valier Heylandt Rak 7.

That night, he and two assistants, Arthur Rudolph and Walter Riedel, were testing an engine that burned alcohol mixed with water and liquid oxygen. No goggles. No fireproof clothing. No concrete wall for cover. Valier sat right in front of the combustion chamber, his face pointed straight into the flame. Rocketry had no money back then, and nobody thought any of that was necessary yet.

The first two test runs that night went fine. Valier wanted one more.

On the third run, while he was adjusting the chamber pressure by hand, the engine exploded.

A piece of metal flew off and tore through his pulmonary artery. Valier bled to death in about ten minutes, right there on the spot. He was the first casualty of the modern rocket age to die doing the work itself.

Arthur Rudolph, standing right next to him, walked away without a scratch. He went on to improve the very engine that had just killed Valier into a safer design. Decades later he led the team that built the Saturn V rocket, the one that put American astronauts on the moon. In 1984, the US Justice Department found out that during World War Two, Rudolph had served as technical director of the Mittelwerk V-2 rocket factory. It was built and run using slave labor from prisoners at the Mittelbau-Dora concentration camp. Thousands of prisoners died there. Rudolph agreed to give up his American citizenship and leave the country for good, in exchange for not being prosecuted.

## 7. The Ship With the Spinning Guns

On the night of September 7th, 1870, off Cape Finisterre in the Bay of Biscay, Captain Cowper Phipps Coles, fifty one years old, was aboard the warship he had designed himself, HMS Captain, in the middle of a sea voyage.

Coles had joined the Royal Navy at age eleven. During the Crimean War, he mounted guns on a raft with a domed shield and used it to shell the Russian town of Taganrog. It worked so well that after the war he patented a rotating gun turret for warships, the first design of its kind in the world. To give the turret the widest possible field of fire, he insisted the ship be built with an extremely low freeboard, sitting almost flush with the water.

The Navy's chief constructor, Edward Reed, had objected to the design since 1868. He warned it would become dangerously unstable in strong wind against its oversized rigging. During construction, the ship came out far heavier than planned. That raised its center of gravity and sank the hull lower than intended. Its maximum angle before it could no longer right itself was down to just 23 degrees. Reed's warnings were overruled after sea trials that spring seemed to go smoothly.

That night, a force six gale kicked up offshore.

Just after midnight, as a new watch came on, the ship began to heel 18 degrees, then lurched twice more to starboard. Before the crew could get the sails down, it rolled over completely and capsized. It floated upside down for three to ten minutes before it went under.

Of about 500 men aboard, only 18 survived. A lifeboat broke free as the ship rolled, and they managed to cling to it. Cowper Coles was among the dead. Britain's First Lord of the Admiralty, Hugh Childers, and the Under Secretary of State for War, Thomas Baring, both lost sons in the disaster.

The ship went down with nearly everyone who had ever believed in his design.

## 8. The Greatest Storm There Ever Was

On the night of November 27th, 1703, Henry Winstanley, fifty nine years old, was inside the lighthouse he had built with his own hands, making a few small repairs, out on the Eddystone Rocks off Plymouth.

Winstanley was an English painter, engineer, and merchant. He was already famous around London for years, thanks to an attraction called Winstanley's Water Works. It combined fireworks, nonstop fountains, and strange automated contraptions, all of his own design. He lost two of his five merchant ships on the Eddystone Rocks. So he paid to build the first lighthouse ever placed there himself, breaking ground in 1696 and finishing in 1698. Nobody knows how many sailors it saved from the fate that had already claimed so many ships. That very first winter, a storm damaged part of it. He reinforced it and finished strengthening the structure by 1699.

He believed completely in what he had built. He had once said he wished he could be inside it during "the greatest storm there ever was."

He got his wish.

That night, one of the most violent storms ever to hit England tore through the south of the country. Nearly 300 Royal Navy ships were destroyed or damaged along the southern coast, and an entire Channel patrol squadron was practically wiped out in one night. The warship HMS Mary alone went down with Rear Admiral Beaumont and 268 sailors aboard. Only one man survived. Across England, at least 8,000 people died that night.

The Eddystone lighthouse was swept away completely. Winstanley and seven others inside all died. Not a single body was ever found.

He got exactly the storm he wished for.

## 9. The Pulley by the Bed

On November 2nd, 1944, at his home in Worthington, Ohio, Thomas Midgley Jr., fifty five years old, lay in his bed.

Midgley was one of the most influential chemists of the twentieth century. In 1921, he discovered that adding tetraethyl lead to gasoline eliminated engine knock. General Motors and Standard Oil went on to form the Ethyl Corporation to mass produce it. Not long after, Midgley himself came down with lead poisoning and had to take almost a year off to recover. But on October 30th, 1924, in front of reporters, he still poured tetraethyl lead over his hands, held a bottle of it to his nose, and inhaled for a full sixty seconds. He claimed he could do it every day without any harm at all. Within the first two months of operation, the Ethyl plant in Bayway poisoned dozens of workers, drove some of them insane, and killed five. The DuPont plant in Deepwater, New Jersey, killed eight more from lead exposure within a year. By the late 1920s, he had moved on to developing Freon, the first CFC refrigerant, later banned worldwide for destroying the ozone layer. Few people in history have done as much damage to the environment and to human health through the work of one person alone.

In 1940, at age 51, he contracted polio and lost the ability to walk.

He designed an elaborate system of ropes and pulleys to lift himself from his bed into his wheelchair without anyone's help.

He got tangled in that same system of ropes. The cords tightened around his neck. Midgley died of strangulation, killed by the last machine he ever built. The coroner ruled it a suicide.

Of everything he ever invented, leaded gasoline, CFCs, the simplest machine of all was the one that actually killed him.
