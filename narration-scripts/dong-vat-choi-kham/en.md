# Animals Play Pranks Too (English narration — approved 2026-07-31)

One night at an aquarium in Germany, the lights over one of the tanks kept blowing out. Third time that week. The maintenance crew checked every wire, every bulb, and found nothing wrong — until someone pulled a night shift and actually watched it happen. An octopus named Otto crawled to the rim of his tank, tilted up, and shot a jet of water straight at the lamp overhead. Short-circuit. Lights out. Exactly like the two times before.

Otto wasn't hungry. Nothing was threatening him. He just didn't like that light glaring down at him, and he'd figured out exactly how to shut it up.

We tend to assume that pranking someone — planning it, timing it, actually enjoying the payoff — takes a fairly sophisticated brain. A sense of humor, even. But what if I told you that a creature with a brain split into eight separate pieces, one running down each arm, whose lineage split from ours over 500 million years ago, pulls pranks with the same gleeful precision as a mischievous kid? You might look at your cat knocking a glass off the table tonight a little differently.

Back to Otto for a second. He's not the only octopus caught misbehaving purely for fun. Researcher Jennifer Mather once watched octopuses in a lab tank repeatedly push a floating object into the water current, just to watch it drift away — then swim over and shove it back in, over and over. Not for food. Not to escape. Just a game they invented themselves. For an invertebrate with no long childhood to "learn how to play" the way mammals do, inventing entertainment — and using it to mess with the humans nearby — is strange enough that scientists file it under a very specific category: genuine play behavior.

If you think that's just one weird octopus, fly out to the mountains of New Zealand, home to a parrot called the kea.

Keas are notorious thieves — unzipping tourists' backpacks, making off with windshield wipers, once even lifting a wallet clean out of someone's pocket before anyone noticed. But here's the detail that actually stopped researchers in their tracks: keas have a specific call they make while playing. In 2017, a team published in the journal Current Biology that simply playing a recording of that call to a group of quiet keas was enough to send them into a play frenzy — chasing each other, wrestling, stealing each other's toys — as if they'd all just heard the same joke. In other words: keas have something close to contagious laughter. Someone starts laughing, everyone joins in. One kea starts a prank, the whole flock piles on.

Sound familiar? It's basically a classroom cracking up because one kid started giggling, and nobody even remembers what was funny.

Now let's shrink things down, to a lab at Humboldt University in Berlin, where scientists tried something that sounds almost silly: teaching rats to play hide-and-seek with a human. And the rats actually learned it. When it was their turn to hide, they'd scurry into a box and go completely silent until they were found. When it was their turn to seek, they'd tear around the room hunting for the researcher — and the moment they found him, they'd leap in the air and immediately bolt off to hide again, angling for one more round. The team recorded bursts of ultrasonic calls, the exact frequency rats only make when they're having a genuinely good time. A game with no food reward, no prize. Played purely because playing felt good.

And if you still need convincing, meet Kelly, a bottlenose dolphin who lived at a marine research facility in the US. Her trainers taught her a simple rule: bring up litter from the pool, get a fish. Kelly learned fast — fast enough that she started hiding a scrap of paper at the bottom of the tank, tearing it into smaller pieces, and turning in exactly one tiny piece per day for a fish, instead of handing over the whole thing at once for a single reward. A dolphin found a loophole in a rule humans invented, and quietly worked it for days before anyone caught on.

So there it is: an octopus messing with a lightbulb, a parrot spreading laughter through its whole flock, rats inventing hide-and-seek, a dolphin gaming a reward system behind her trainers' backs. Four completely different brains, four completely different bodies — and all four apparently enjoy getting away with something.

At this point, someone's probably raising an eyebrow. Aren't we reading a bit too much into this? Slapping a "sense of humor" onto anything an animal does that happens to look funny? Fair question — and scientists studying play behavior ask it of themselves too. There's actually a well-known checklist, developed by ethologist Gordon Burghardt, for telling real play apart from random reflexes: the behavior has to repeat, it can't serve any immediate survival purpose, and it has to look like it's giving the animal something pleasurable. Otto didn't zap that light once and stop — he kept doing it. The rats didn't hide once and call it done — they kept demanding another round. These behaviors check every box on that list, even though none of us can actually sit an octopus down and ask, "hey, was that fun?"

And here's the part I find genuinely fascinating. Octopuses and humans split onto separate evolutionary paths more than half a billion years ago — so long ago that whatever ancestor we shared barely had anything resembling a brain at all. And yet both branches, evolving completely independently, arrived at something strikingly similar: the sheer enjoyment of teasing, of bending the rules, of catching someone off guard. Which suggests mischief probably isn't some lucky quirk that happened to land on humans alone. It looks more like a solution nature has stumbled onto again and again, in wildly different corners of the evolutionary tree — because, it turns out, it's useful. Learning to joke, to play, to test the rules in a low-stakes setting, is exactly how a brain rehearses for the moments that actually matter later.

Which brings us back to Otto one last time. The night he fried that lightbulb, the aquarium staff didn't punish him. They just moved him to a different tank and repositioned the light — and quietly admitted something very human about the whole situation: this wasn't a technical glitch. This was the single most impressive prank an eight-armed creature has ever pulled on an annoying light fixture.

So tonight, if your cat knocks a pen off the desk while staring you dead in the eye, or your dog suddenly "forgets" the word "drop it" just to score one more chase around the living room — don't write it off as random instinct. There's a decent chance you've just been picked as tonight's target. And somewhere out there, an octopus is eyeing that light above its tank, already planning the next blackout.
