## 1. Carlina White — The Baby Who Solved Her Own Kidnapping

In the summer of 1987, in New York City, Joy White and Carl Tyson welcomed their first daughter, Carlina. Nineteen days later the baby spiked a fever of 104 degrees, and the young couple rushed her to Harlem Hospital.

While they waited, a woman in a nurse's uniform sat beside them and comforted the frightened parents. Joy never forgot how kind she was.

That woman did not work at the hospital. Investigators later learned she'd been seen loitering around the building for three weeks.

At around two in the morning on August 4th, right as the hospital changed shifts, someone pulled the IV line out of baby Carlina's arm. A guard reported seeing a woman matching that description walk out the doors around three thirty, carrying an infant. When Joy went back to the room, the crib was empty.

The woman's name was Ann Pettway. She took a train roughly sixty two miles to Bridgeport, Connecticut, and raised the baby as her own under the name Nejdra Nance. Everyone at home called her Netty.


Netty grew up like any other kid, but by her teenage years something felt wrong. She looked nothing like her mother, and she could never get a Social Security card.

In 2005, pregnant and needing a birth certificate for health insurance, Netty got a document from Pettway that a clerk at Connecticut's vital records office told her flatly was forged. Cornered, Pettway admitted she wasn't Netty's real mother, claiming a drug addict had abandoned the baby with her.

In 2010, twenty three and living in Atlanta, Netty found the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children's website and widened her search there. She found a photo of an infant kidnapped in 1987. What stopped her cold wasn't that the baby looked like her. It was that the baby looked exactly like her own daughter. That December, she called the center's hotline.


A few weeks later, Netty called Joy White. The two of them spoke by phone several times before any DNA results came back. In early 2011 she flew to New York to meet her biological parents for the first time in twenty three years. Just as she was boarding her flight home to Atlanta, police called to confirm the DNA was a match.

Ann Pettway turned herself in to the FBI in Bridgeport on January 23rd, 2011, and was sentenced on July 30th, 2012, to twelve years. At sentencing, Carl Tyson told her the sentence should have been twenty three years, exactly what she'd stolen from him. Joy White said only that she was broken into a million pieces.

As for Carlina, after finally learning her real name, she chose to keep going by Netty, the one thing about her identity that had always belonged to her alone.

## 2. Fusako Sano — The Room With a Door That Was Never Locked

On November 13th, 1990, in the town of Sanjo in Niigata Prefecture, Japan, a nine year old girl named Fusako Sano watched her school's baseball game and started walking home. On the way, a twenty eight year old man named Nobuyuki Sato forced her into his car.

He drove thirty four miles to his home in Kashiwazaki, took her upstairs, and she stayed there for nine years and two months.

For the first several months he kept her tied up, forced her to record horse races on television, and used a stun gun on her when she got it wrong. He threatened her with knives, gave her men's clothes to wear, cut her hair himself, and rarely let her bathe. For most of nine years her only connection to the outside world was a radio, and she wasn't allowed a television until the final year.


Here's the detail that sets this case apart. Sato's mother, in her seventies, lived on the floor right below them for all nine years, and cooked most of the meals carried upstairs.

This needs a little context. In Japan, it's completely normal for a grown man to live with his elderly mother in the same house. Sato turned violent whenever she tried to climb the stairs, so she stopped. Police later believed she knew someone was up there.

What she actually did was call the local public health center. Not the police. She called first in January of 1996, saying her son had turned strange and violent. She called again on January 12th and January 19th of 2000. Officials finally showed up at the house on Friday, January 28th, 2000.

Sato flew into a rage loud enough that police were called in. In the middle of the chaos, a thin, pale young woman came down the stairs and told the officers her own name. She was nineteen.


She was so thin she could barely walk, dehydrated, jaundiced, her skin pale from years with almost no sunlight. Doctors noted she behaved like a child, frozen at the age she'd been taken. When her mother arrived, she didn't recognize her own daughter.

Sato had already been convicted in 1989 of assaulting a different girl, but his name had somehow vanished from the criminal record, so in nine years of searching police never once considered him a suspect. His house sat six hundred and sixty feet from a police substation.

The case became a national scandal in Japan. On the day of the rescue, the Niigata police chief was out playing mahjong instead of overseeing the operation, and he resigned a month later. Sato got fourteen years, was released in 2015, and was found dead in a Chiba apartment a couple of years after that.

But here's the detail Japanese police never explained. The door leading down those stairs was never locked. Not for one single day of the nine years and two months.

Fusako Sano lives quietly today, helping her family with their rice fields and photographing flowers. She has very few friends and prefers to walk alone. In 2007, her father drowned in a pond right in front of her during an outing together.

## 3. Hiroo Onoda — The Soldier Who Was Dead for Fifteen Years

On December 26th, 1944, a twenty two year old Japanese intelligence officer named Hiroo Onoda received orders to report to Lubang Island in the Philippines. He'd trained at the Nakano School, Japan's academy for guerrilla warfare, and his orders were explicit. Under no circumstances was he to surrender, and under no circumstances was he to take his own life.

He followed those orders for twenty nine years.

American and Filipino forces landed on the island on February 28th, 1945. Onoda and three other soldiers retreated into the mountains. Japan surrendered that August 15th. The four men in the jungle didn't believe it.

That makes more sense with a little context. Planes dropped leaflets saying the war was over, but to an officer trained specifically to spot enemy propaganda, a leaflet telling him to lay down his weapon looked exactly like propaganda was supposed to look. And no one with the authority to cancel his orders had ever done so.


They lived on bananas and coconuts and raided rice stores for food. Over the years, Onoda's small group killed around thirty people they believed were enemy soldiers.

The first to leave was Private Yuichi Akatsu, who split off in 1949 and surrendered in 1950. Corporal Shoichi Shimada was killed in a shootout on May 7th, 1954. Private Kinshichi Kozuka was shot dead on October 19th, 1972, during a raid on a rice store. After that, Onoda was alone.

The Japanese government had already declared him dead back in 1959. His family had held a funeral.


In 1974, a young Japanese man named Norio Suzuki dropped out of college to travel the world. He told friends he was looking for three things, in this exact order. Lieutenant Onoda, a panda, and the Abominable Snowman.

On February 20th, 1974, after four days of searching Lubang, Suzuki found the first item on his list.

Onoda talked with him but refused to leave, saying he would only lay down his weapon if his own commanding officer ordered him to. Suzuki brought photographs back to Japan, and the government tracked down Major Yoshimi Taniguchi, Onoda's old commander, now working as a bookseller, and flew him to Lubang.

On March 9th, 1974, deep in the jungle, Taniguchi read Onoda a formal order stating that his army had ceased all combat operations and Lieutenant Onoda was relieved of duty.

The next day, Onoda walked out of the jungle still wearing his original uniform, intact after twenty nine years. He handed over his officer's sword, his still functioning rifle, five hundred rounds of ammunition, several hand grenades, and a dagger his mother had given him in 1944. He'd held out for twenty eight years, six months, and eight days after his country surrendered.

Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos granted him a full pardon, since Onoda genuinely believed the war was still going on when those killings happened.

Onoda came home to a massive media frenzy. He lasted about a year before moving to Brazil to raise cattle, and spent years there before eventually returning to Japan. He died on January 16th, 2014, at ninety one.

## 4. Tanya Kach — The Girl Who Walked Through Her Own Hometown

McKeesport, Pennsylvania is an old steel town outside Pittsburgh, the kind of place where everyone recognizes everyone. In February of 1996, a fourteen year old eighth grader named Tanya Nicole Kach vanished from there, and her picture went up all over town. She became what Americans call a milk carton kid, her face printed on cartons in grocery stores nationwide.

For the next ten years, Tanya lived a few minutes' drive from her own father's house.

The man holding her was Thomas Hose, thirty eight years old, the security guard at her own middle school. He'd spent months befriending her, and eventually convinced her to run away.


Hose lived with his parents and his son, and kept Tanya hidden in his second floor bedroom. For the first four years she wasn't allowed to leave that room, not once. She couldn't even use the bathroom downstairs, so she used a bucket instead. Three other people lived in that house for four straight years, and none of them ever came upstairs.

In 2000, Hose did something bolder. He gave her a new identity, a young woman named Nikki, and introduced her to his parents as his girlfriend, moving in for good. Nobody asked a single question.

From then on, Tanya was allowed outside under a strict curfew. She walked around the very town where her missing person flyers had once hung, and got a job at a local deli under the name Nikki. Neighbors got to know her face. Nobody recognized the girl who'd vanished at fourteen, now in her twenties.


The deli was owned by a man named Joe Sparico. For months, he and his wife talked with the young woman who called herself Nikki, and slowly sensed something was very wrong.

On March 21st, 2006, Tanya broke down crying at work and told Joe her name wasn't really Nikki, that her real name was Tanya Kach, and that if he looked up missing children online he would find her photo. She asked him to call the police to the house.

The Sparicos called their son, a retired police officer who remembered the case. Within hours, officers surrounded Hose's home. Tanya was twenty four years old.

Thomas Hose pleaded guilty in 2007 to abusing Tanya and holding her against her will, and received five to fifteen years. He served the entire fifteen, was released in 2022, and had to register as a sex offender.

Tanya sued the agencies that should have found her. Every lawsuit was dismissed. She later wrote a memoir, and titled it after the very thing that had once put her face in front of the whole country. A milk carton.

## 5. Natascha Kampusch — Three Thousand Ninety Six Days Under the Garage

On the morning of March 2nd, 1998, a ten year old girl named Natascha Kampusch left her family's apartment in Vienna, Austria, to walk to school. She never got there. A witness reported seeing her dragged into a white van.

The man in that van was Wolfgang Priklopil, a telecommunications technician living in the town of Strasshof, about thirty minutes outside Vienna. He brought her to his house, opened a small steel hatch hidden behind a cupboard, led her down a narrow staircase beneath his garage, and shut the door.

The room below measured about fifty four square feet. No windows. Completely soundproofed.


Priklopil controlled everything, even the electricity, plunging her into total darkness whenever he wanted. He installed a two way intercom to give her orders at any hour, starved her, forced her to work, and told her the doors were rigged with explosives and that he always carried a gun, so if she ever ran, she would die.

For eight years she grew up in that cellar, but barely. When she finally got out, she was only six inches taller than the day she was taken, and weighed one hundred and six pounds, barely more than an average ten year old.


On August 23rd, 2006, Priklopil told her to vacuum out his van in the garden. Partway through, his phone rang, and because the vacuum was too loud, he stepped away to take the call.

Natascha left the vacuum running and ran. She sprinted roughly six hundred and fifty feet through neighboring yards, hopping fences along the way. After about five minutes, she knocked on a stranger's window and told the woman inside, I am Natascha Kampusch.

Police arrived at 1:04 in the afternoon and confirmed her identity with a scar on her body, with her own passport, which had been sitting in the cellar the entire time, and later with a DNA test. That same evening, knowing police were closing in, Priklopil threw himself in front of an oncoming train near a station in Vienna.

She had spent three thousand and ninety six days in that cellar.

A few days later, Natascha Kampusch, now eighteen, went to the medical examiner's office to see the body of the man who had held her captive. He was buried under a false name, in a plain wooden coffin, with exactly two people standing at the grave.

Years later, Natascha Kampusch became the legal owner of that very house. She still owns it today. The cellar beneath the garage was filled in with concrete in 2011.

## 6. Steven Stayner — The Boy Who Walked Another Boy to the Police Station

On December 4th, 1972, a seven year old boy named Steven Stayner was walking home from school in Merced, California, when a man approached, said he was collecting donations for a church, and asked if Steven wanted a ride home. Waiting in the car was a second man, Kenneth Parnell, already convicted once of abusing a child.

Parnell took the boy to a cabin in Catheys Valley, just a few hundred feet from Steven's own grandfather's house. He renamed the boy Dennis Parnell, keeping his real middle name and birthdate. Thirteen days after the kidnapping he began abusing him, telling Steven that a court had granted him custody because Steven's real parents were too poor to raise so many kids.


This is what makes the case different from every other one on this list. Steven Stayner was never locked up.

He lived openly, moved with Parnell through Santa Rosa and Comptche, went to school, had friends. Neighbors knew him as Mr. Parnell's son. For seven years, a boy listed as missing in California police files walked around in broad daylight, and no adult ever questioned it.

Then he got older, and Parnell lost interest in him. On February 14th, 1980, Parnell and an accomplice kidnapped a five year old boy named Timothy White in Ukiah, dragging him into a car while he screamed and struggled. Steven, now fourteen, watched it happen, and saw himself from seven years earlier.


That night, while Parnell was at his job as a security guard, Steven took Timmy and ran. The two boys hitchhiked nearly forty miles back to Ukiah. Once there, Steven couldn't find Timmy's house, so he did the only thing left to do. He walked the boy straight into the police station.

His handwritten statement that night began with these words. My name is Steven Stainer, misspelling his own last name. I am fourteen years of age. I don't know my true birthdate, but I use April 18th, 1965.

By the next day, both boys were back with their families.


Kenneth Parnell was convicted in 1981 of kidnapping both boys, got seven years, and was paroled after five. He was never prosecuted for the years of abuse, blocked by the statute of limitations, and a woman who had lived with him and taken part in it was never arrested.

Steven came home and couldn't adjust. Under Parnell he'd been allowed to smoke and drink since childhood, got almost no counseling, refused to talk about what happened, was bullied at school, dropped out, and started drinking heavily. His own parents eventually asked him to move out.

At twenty he married and had two kids, worked with child safety organizations, and spoke to students about protecting themselves. He was working at a pizza shop in Merced when, on September 16th, 1989, a car turned into the path of his motorcycle. The driver fled the scene, and Steven died of head trauma at twenty four. The driver was later identified, got three months for hit and run, and was found not guilty of manslaughter.

Five hundred people came to his funeral. One of the pallbearers was Timothy White, who had just turned fourteen, the exact age Steven had been the night he walked him into that police station.

Steven also had an older brother named Cary. In 1999, ten years after Steven's death, Cary Stayner murdered four women near Yosemite National Park and was sentenced to death in 2002.

Years later, Merced considered naming a park after Steven, the boy who freed himself and saved another child. They decided against it, worried that hearing the name Stayner, people wouldn't think of Steven first.
