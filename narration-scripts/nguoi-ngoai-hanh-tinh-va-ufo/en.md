## 1. Travis Walton — Five Days Nobody Could Explain

On November 5th, 1975, in the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest near the town of Heber, Arizona, a seven man logging crew was driving back after a day's work, the light already fading. The crew's foreman was Mike Rogers, twenty eight years old. Riding with him was Travis Walton, twenty two, along with five other loggers.

They were racing a deadline. Their forest clearing contract was already late, and if it wasn't finished by November 10th, the crew would be hit with a twenty five hundred dollar penalty and lose the right to bid on future contracts.

Partway down the road, the whole truck saw a strange light hovering above the trees. Travis Walton got out and walked into the forest toward it.


According to the other six men, a beam of light struck Travis and he dropped to the ground. Panicked, the crew drove off immediately, certain he was dead. Fifteen minutes later, when they worked up the nerve to go back, both Travis and the light were gone.

At 7:45 that evening, the crew reported it to police. A massive search of the forest began and ran for days without turning up a single trace.

Police started suspecting the six men themselves. On November 11th, all six sat for polygraph tests. According to the examiner from the Arizona Department of Public Safety, five of the six were found truthful about not having harmed Travis. One man's result came back inconclusive.


That same day, five days after he vanished, Travis's sister got a collect call from a payphone in Heber. It was Travis. A family friend drove out to get him. The operator recognized his name and alerted the local sheriff. A deputy reached him by 2 a.m.

The doctor who examined Travis found a small puncture wound on the inside of his right elbow, consistent with a needle mark, estimated at twenty four to forty eight hours old.

On November 22nd, on a Phoenix television station, Travis said he woke up in a room that looked like a hospital, with three short, bald creatures standing around him. He said there was another being that looked more human, wearing a helmet. After that he remembered nothing until he found himself lying on a highway.


At the time, the tabloid National Enquirer was offering a hundred thousand dollar prize to anyone who could prove extraterrestrial life. After Travis's story broke, the paper gave the crew its "Best Case of the Year" award, worth five thousand dollars, based on the polygraph results.

Plenty of skeptics have argued the whole thing was staged so the crew could dodge their contract penalty, since the contract had a clause waiving the fine in the event of an act of God.

## 2. Val Johnson — The Deputy and the Light on the Highway

At 2 a.m. on August 27th, 1979, near the town of Stephen, Minnesota, deputy sheriff Val Johnson was patrolling an empty stretch of road. He reported seeing a beam of light above the highway, and that the beam suddenly rushed toward his car at incredible speed.

He said his whole squad car was engulfed in light, he heard glass breaking, and then he blacked out.

When his colleagues found him, the squad car was sitting sideways across the road. He'd been unconscious for thirty nine minutes. The windshield was shattered, one headlight was destroyed, the red emergency light was damaged, and the radio antenna was bent.


Johnson had a habit of syncing his wristwatch to his squad car's dashboard clock before every shift, down to the minute. When he came to after the incident, he found both timepieces, his watch and the car clock, were exactly fourteen minutes slow.

The doctor who examined him noted bruising and eye irritation that looked, in his words, like welder's burns. The sheriff's department filed an official report on the incident, one that's still on record today.

On September 11th, 1979, Val Johnson appeared on ABC's Good Morning America.

A skeptical researcher accused him of staging the whole thing and damaging his own car. Johnson refused to take a polygraph test, saying he didn't want to satisfy what he called people's morbid curiosity. The case has never been officially solved, and the original police report still reads exactly as he wrote it that night.

## 3. Cash-Landrum — The Diamond Over a Texas Backroad

Around 9 p.m. on December 29th, 1980, on a lonely two lane road through the woods near Dayton, Texas, about thirty five miles from Houston Intercontinental Airport, three people were driving home. Betty Cash, fifty one, was behind the wheel. With her was Vickie Landrum, fifty seven, a devout born again Christian, and Vickie's seven year old grandson, Colby Landrum.

They spotted a massive diamond shaped object hovering just above treetop level, flames shooting out from underneath it in the shape of a huge cone. At first they thought it was an airplane.


Then they saw roughly twenty three military helicopters, identified as CH-47 Chinooks, flying in formation around the object. The whole encounter lasted about twenty minutes.

Within hours, all three of them were nauseated, vomiting, and weak.

Betty Cash got hit the hardest. Her eyes burned like she had a bad sunburn, her skin blistered and peeled off in large patches, and her hair fell out in clumps. She was hospitalized from January 2nd to the 19th, 1981, then hospitalized again for another fifteen days after that. Vickie and Colby had milder symptoms that still lingered for weeks, weakness, skin sores, hair loss. By April of 1981, Vickie had developed a cataract.


With the backing of two US senators, the three of them sued the federal government for twenty million dollars. An Army lieutenant colonel investigated the case in 1982 and found no evidence the helicopters belonged to the US military, though he noted the witnesses were credible. On August 21st, 1986, a federal court dismissed the case, since no government agency could be identified as owning either the object or the helicopters.

Betty Cash was diagnosed with breast cancer in 1983. She died on December 29th, 1998, exactly eighteen years to the night after the encounter. Vickie Landrum died in 2007, at eighty three.

## 4. Rendlesham Forest — Britain's Roswell

At around 3 a.m. on December 26th, 1980, at the east gate of RAF Woodbridge in Suffolk, England, a base run by the US Air Force, security personnel reported lights descending into the nearby Rendlesham Forest.

Believing a plane had gone down, servicemen went in to investigate. According to the official memo written afterward, they witnessed a glowing object, metallic in appearance, with colored lights. It seemed to move through the trees. Animals on a nearby farm went into a panic.

Local police were called after 4 a.m. but reported seeing nothing but the Orford Ness lighthouse, miles off on the coast.

After daybreak, the servicemen found three small impressions in the ground, burn marks, and broken branches.


Two nights later, on December 28th, Lieutenant Colonel Charles Halt, the base's deputy commander, personally led a team back to the site. Using radiation detectors, they measured levels well above the normal background, with similar readings turning up at a separate spot over half a mile away.

That night, a flashing red light appeared on the horizon, and three star like objects showed up in the sky. The brightest one hovered for two to three hours and appeared to beam light down toward the ground. Halt recorded the entire thing live on a handheld cassette recorder, a tape that later became known simply as the Halt tape.

The incident only became public years later, through a freedom of information request. The UK Ministry of Defence stated it posed no threat to national security.

Skeptics eventually offered an explanation. A meteor burning up in the atmosphere that same night, the Orford Ness lighthouse flashing every five seconds, and bright stars misidentified in the dark. But the official US military memo describing a metallic object is still sitting in the record exactly as written.

## 5. Ariel School — 62 Children Telling the Same Story

At 10 a.m. on September 16th, 1994, at a private school called Ariel in the town of Ruwa, Zimbabwe, about fourteen miles from the capital, Harare, students were out for mid morning break. Every teacher was inside in a meeting. No adult was out on the grounds.

Over about fifteen minutes, sixty two students between the ages of six and twelve would later say they watched one or more silver discs descend and land on a field near the school. One to four beings stepped out, with waxy looking skin and unusually large eyes, and moved toward the children. Some ran. The older ones stayed to watch.

When the kids told their teachers, most weren't believed. Parents only found out when their children came home talking about it, and showed up at the school the next morning demanding answers.


On September 19th, a BBC reporter came to film interviews with the students and staff. Afterward he said he could handle a war zone, but he couldn't handle this.

On September 20th, a UFO researcher interviewed the children in small groups of four to six, and had them draw what they'd seen. The accounts, this researcher found, were remarkably consistent.

In November of 1994, John Mack, a Harvard psychiatry professor and Pulitzer Prize winning author, flew out to interview each child individually. In those interviews, some of the children said the beings had "spoken" to them, not with words but through some kind of telepathy, warning that people were destroying the planet. One eleven year old girl put it this way. I think they want people to know that we're actually making harm on this world, and we mustn't get too technologed.

The case became one of the most famous UFO sightings to come out of Africa. Many of the witnesses, now adults, still appear in documentaries decades later and stand by exactly what they said as children. In 2023, a former student named Dallyn went on Netflix claiming he'd made the whole thing up, that it started with him calling a shiny rock a UFO. His claim directly contradicts his own statements from 2008, and every other witness's account.

## 6. Betty and Barney Hill — America's First Alien Abduction

On the night of September 19th, 1961, Barney Hill, thirty nine, a postal worker, and his wife Betty Hill, forty two, a social worker, were driving home to Portsmouth, New Hampshire, from a vacation in Montreal. One detail matters here for understanding 1961 America. Barney was Black and Betty was white, an interracial couple, and both of them were active in the civil rights movement.

Around 10:30 that night, on Route 3, Betty spotted a light in the sky moving in a way that didn't make sense. They pulled over at a scenic lookout near Twin Mountain. Through binoculars, Betty saw an oddly shaped object, flashing multicolored lights, crossing in front of the moon. Barney first assumed it was a commercial airliner, until it suddenly dropped altitude and came straight toward them.


The object kept descending until it hovered silently just eighty to a hundred feet above their car. Barney got out with the binoculars and said he saw somewhere between eight and eleven humanoid figures looking out through the craft's windows, dressed in glossy black uniforms and caps. He felt like one of them sent him an order. Stay where you are.

Terrified, Barney ran back to the car. They both heard a strange beeping sound, and then fell into a daze.

When they came to, they were near the town of Concord, about thirty five miles from where they remembered being. A drive that should have taken four hours had taken seven. Neither of them could remember much of that stretch of road at all.

Back home, they found their watches had stopped running for good. The binocular strap was torn. Barney's dress shoes were scuffed at the toes. Betty's dress had a torn hem and zipper, with a strange pink powder dusted across the fabric. Shiny concentric circles had appeared on the trunk of their car that hadn't been there before, and when they held a compass near them, the needle spun wildly.

On October 25th, 1961, the couple formally reported the incident to Pease Air Force Base.


Ten days after the encounter, Betty started having vivid dreams, five nights running, of being led through the woods by two small men.

By late 1961, UFO researchers recalculating the timeline pointed out an unexplained three hour gap, and the Hills had no answer for it. They were advised to try hypnosis to recover the missing memories.

Starting in February of 1964, the two of them underwent separate hypnosis sessions with psychiatrist Benjamin Simon, each interviewed independently, with neither knowing what the other had said.

Under hypnosis, Barney recalled that after the beeping sound, he saw six men blocking a dirt road, three of whom walked toward the car. Both of them, he said, were led up a ramp into a metal craft and separated into two different rooms.

In his room, Barney was most fixated on the beings' eyes. Those eyes, he said. They're right there in my head. He said he heard a mumbling language he couldn't understand, and that they communicated through something he called thought transference, a term he'd never even heard before that session. He said the beings seemed surprised to discover his teeth could be removed and put back, since Barney wore dentures.

Betty, in her own room, described two beings, one she called a leader and one an examiner. The examiner pushed a long needle into her navel, causing a burst of intense pain, and told her it was a pregnancy test. When she cried out, the leader waved his hand in front of her eyes, and the pain vanished instantly. That same leader, she said, showed her a three dimensional star map, calling it their trade and exploration routes.

What struck Dr. Simon and the researchers most wasn't any single detail. It was that despite being interviewed completely separately, with neither spouse knowing what the other had said, their accounts matched on most of the core facts. Being led aboard. Being separated into rooms. Even the same phrase for how the beings communicated.


During one hypnosis session, Betty sketched the star map she said she'd seen. In 1968, an elementary school teacher and amateur astronomer named Marjorie Fish read the account and spent years building a three dimensional model of nearby sunlike stars out of thread and beads, using distances published in the 1969 Gliese Star Catalogue.

After testing thousands of different vantage points, she found exactly one that matched Betty's sketch, viewed from the binary star system Zeta Reticuli, about thirty nine light years from Earth. The finding became known as the Zeta Reticuli incident and spread through UFO research circles.

But years later, more precise distance measurements from the Hipparcos satellite in the early 1990s showed Fish's model had errors, and she herself publicly withdrew the theory. Astronomer Carl Sagan also demonstrated that without the extra lines someone had drawn connecting the dots, Betty's star map didn't match any real cluster of stars at all.

The story stayed private for years until October 25th, 1965, when the Boston Traveler ran a front page piece titled "UFO Chiller: Did THEY Seize Couple?", revealing that the couple had undergone hypnosis. In 1966, the book The Interrupted Journey was published, telling the whole story, and became a bestseller.

Barney Hill died in 1969 of a cerebral hemorrhage, at forty six. Betty Hill died in 2004 of cancer, at eighty five, having never remarried. All of her papers, tapes, and notes are permanently archived at the University of New Hampshire. In 2011, the state of New Hampshire put up a historical marker at the exact spot where the object first appeared over the road.
