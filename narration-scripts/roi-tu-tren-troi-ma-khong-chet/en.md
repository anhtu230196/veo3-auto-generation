## 1. The Woman Who Wasn't Supposed to Be There

On January 26th, 1972, Vesna Vulović, twenty two years old, boarded JAT Flight 367 from Copenhagen to Zagreb as a flight attendant.

She was never supposed to be on that plane. The Yugoslav airline had mixed her up with another flight attendant also named Vesna when they wrote the schedule. Vulović didn't complain. The mistake got her a trip to Denmark, a country she had never seen.

The plane took off from Copenhagen and climbed to cruising altitude.

Forty six minutes into the flight, a bomb hidden in the baggage compartment went off.

The DC-9 broke into pieces at 33,330 feet. Most of the passengers were sucked out into open air. All twenty seven other people on board died.

Vulović was in the tail section, standing next to a serving cart. When the fuselage tore open, that cart slammed across her back and pinned her inside the tail instead of letting her get pulled out like everyone else. The shell of the tail section stayed wrapped around her the whole way down.

That section came down on a heavily wooded, snow covered hillside near the village of Srbská Kamenice in Czechoslovakia. The trees and the deep snow absorbed part of the impact.

There was one more factor, and it was inside her own body. Vulović had unusually low blood pressure. Her doctors believed it made her black out almost immediately when the plane came apart, and that it kept her heart from rupturing when she hit the ground.

She survived. Fractured skull. Bleeding on the brain. Two broken legs. Three broken vertebrae, one of them crushed completely. A fractured pelvis and several broken ribs. She was paralyzed from the waist down.

The man who found her was a villager named Bruno Honke. He heard her screaming inside the wreckage. Honke had been a medic in the Second World War. He knew how to stop the bleeding, and he kept doing it until the rescue crews arrived.

She woke up after days in a coma. She stayed in a hospital in Prague until March 12th, 1972, then transferred to Belgrade and stayed there until June. At first she could only move her left leg. Within ten months she could walk again, but her spine had been twisted, and she walked with a limp for the rest of her life. She never remembered a single thing about the explosion.

In 1985, Guinness World Records recognized her for the highest fall ever survived without a parachute. The man who handed her the certificate was Paul McCartney.

In 2009, two journalists in Prague published research claiming the plane actually broke apart at around 2,600 feet after being shot down by mistake by the Czechoslovak Air Force, and that the six mile fall was a cover story. The Czech Civil Aviation Authority rejected it. One of the two journalists later admitted their evidence was only circumstantial. Guinness kept the record.

Vulović, who could not remember one second of the fall, called the theories nonsense.

## 2. The Man With No Parachute

On the night of March 24th, 1944, Nicholas Alkemade, twenty one years old, was sitting in the tail turret of a Lancaster bomber coded DS664, on the way home to England after a raid on Berlin.

Alkemade was a tail gunner with 115 Squadron of the Royal Air Force. The tail turret was the most dangerous seat on a bomber and also the tightest. It was so tight that a gunner could not wear his parachute while sitting in it. Alkemade's parachute hung inside the fuselage, several feet behind him.

East of Schmallenberg, a German Ju 88 night fighter attacked the Lancaster. The bomber caught fire and started spinning out of control.

Alkemade climbed back for his parachute. It had already burned.

He had two options. Burn to death inside the aircraft, or jump without a chute. He picked the second one, because he figured hitting the ground would be faster than burning.

He jumped at about 18,000 feet.

His fall was broken by the tops of pine trees and then by a thick layer of snow on the ground. He lay there, and realized he could move his arms and his legs. His only injury was a sprained knee.

German troops captured him and handed him to the Gestapo. The Gestapo did not believe a man had jumped from 18,000 feet with no parachute. They suspected he was a spy who had jumped normally and hidden his gear. Alkemade told them to go find his harness, because if he had ever opened a chute the harness would show it. The Germans found the harness. There were no marks on it. In the wreck of the Lancaster about twenty miles away, the ripcord handle and cable were still sitting in their stowage. They gave him a certificate confirming the whole thing.

After the war, Alkemade went home to Loughborough and took a job in a chemical plant. There he was electrocuted while siphoning a chlorine producing liquid. His gas mask came loose, and he breathed chlorine for fifteen minutes before anyone heard him shouting. Not long after, a pipe burst and sprayed industrial sulfuric acid across his face and arms. He dove headfirst into a forty gallon drum of limewash to neutralize it and walked away with first degree burns.

He lived another forty one years and died in Cornwall in 1987, working as a furniture salesman.

## 3. Eight Minutes

On August 24th, 1981, Larisa Savitskaya, twenty years old, was sitting next to her husband on an An-24 flight from Komsomolsk-on-Amur to Blagoveshchensk, heading home from their honeymoon.

Savitskaya was a teaching student. She and her husband Vladimir studied at the same school. They had just gotten married and had been visiting his relatives.

At 3:21 in the afternoon, at 17,000 feet near Zavitinsk, the An-24 collided with a Tu-16K military bomber. The top of the passenger plane and both of its wings were torn away. Its propeller sliced open the bomber's fuselage.

Vladimir died instantly in the seat beside her.

The impact threw Savitskaya into the aisle. She remembered an Italian film she had seen about a plane crash survivor, and she copied what she saw. She grabbed the armrests and pressed herself down low between the rows. The piece of fuselage she was in fell for about eight minutes. Its shape made it drop slower than it should have, and it came down into a stand of birch trees. The flexible trunks absorbed part of the impact.

Thirty seven people on that flight died. Savitskaya lived.

She had multiple spinal injuries, broken ribs, a broken arm, a broken leg, a concussion, and several teeth knocked out. She got up and walked. She found a piece of the fuselage to shelter under and spent three days in the taiga drinking rainwater. Search helicopters passed overhead and she waved a red seat cover at them, but nobody spotted her. On the third day she took a pack of cigarettes out of Vladimir's jacket and started smoking, and it was the smoke that finally showed the search team where she was.

By the time they found her, the state had already dug a grave with her name on it, based on the passenger list.

A medical board ruled her injuries were not severe enough to qualify for disability status. The Soviet state paid her 75 rubles in compensation. The average monthly wage at the time was 178 rubles. Guinness lists it as the smallest payout ever awarded for a plane crash.

## 4. The Man Who Chose Not to Pull

On January 21st, 1942, Lieutenant Ivan Chisov of the Soviet Air Force was flying aboard his bomber when German fighters attacked it.

Chisov was a bombardier navigator. His aircraft was hit and badly damaged, and he had to bail out at around 23,000 feet.

He had a parachute. He deliberately did not open it.

The air battle was still going on all around him, and a man hanging under an open canopy in that airspace is a target that cannot move. Chisov's plan was to free fall below the fighting, get out of sight of the German planes, and only then pull the cord.

At that altitude, the air is too thin. Chisov blacked out before he could carry out the second half of the plan.

He free fell almost the entire 23,000 feet. The ground below was buried in deep snow. He struck the steep wall of a ravine at an angle, then tumbled and slid down the snow face until he came to a stop at the bottom.

He lived. Multiple spinal injuries, a broken pelvis, and a broken leg.

Chisov asked to return to combat flying. His request was denied. He spent the rest of the war as an instructor, training bombardier navigators to fly the missions he no longer could.

## 5. He Filmed His Own Fall

In December 2006, Michael Holmes, twenty five years old, jumped out of a plane at 13,000 feet over Lake Taupo, New Zealand, to film a group of ten skydivers.

Holmes was a skydiving instructor with around 7,000 jumps behind him. That day he was wearing a camera mounted on his helmet to record the jump for the customers.

His main parachute tangled and never opened. He dropped into a violent uncontrolled spin.

He cut away and pulled his reserve. The reserve failed too.

The camera on his helmet was still running. It recorded everything that came next. Holmes later described the moment. "When the second parachute didn't open, I realized it was all over. I was going to die. You don't have much time to say goodbye. I just said, I'm going to die."

He hit a blackberry bush at Five Mile Bay, less than 300 feet from a parking lot. The bush absorbed most of the impact.

He survived with a punctured lung and a broken ankle.

At Waikato Hospital, according to the nurses, he spent his recovery cracking jokes and giving everyone a hard time.

## 6. Through the Roof of the Train Station

On January 3rd, 1943, Sergeant Alan Magee, twenty three years old, was crouched in the ball turret of a B-17 named Snap Crackle Pop, flying his seventh mission, bombing the German U-boat pens at Saint-Nazaire in France.

Magee was a ball turret gunner. The ball turret was a sphere of glass and steel slung under the belly of the bomber, small enough that a gunner had to curl up to fit inside, and far too small to wear a parachute in.

German fighters shot away part of the B-17's right wing. The bomber went into a violent spin. Magee was hit by shrapnel but managed to get out of the turret.

His parachute had already been destroyed by the same shrapnel.

He was thrown clear of the aircraft at about 22,000 feet, and he blacked out almost immediately from lack of oxygen.

He fell more than four miles and went straight through the glass roof of the Saint-Nazaire railway station. The glass absorbed most of the impact. They found him alive on the station floor with 28 shrapnel wounds, a broken nose, damaged eyes, a punctured lung, kidney damage, a broken right leg, and a right arm nearly severed from his body.

A German military doctor saved that arm. Magee later repeated what the man told him. "We are enemies, but I am first a doctor, and I will do my best to save your arm."

Magee stayed a prisoner of war until May 1945 and received the Air Medal and the Purple Heart. After the war he earned his pilot's license and went to work in the airline industry.

Exactly fifty years after he fell, on January 3rd, 1993, the people of Saint-Nazaire put up a six foot memorial to him and his crew.

## 7. The Fire Ant Mound

On September 25th, 1999, Joan Murray, forty seven years old, a bank executive from Charlotte, North Carolina, jumped from 14,500 feet on a weekend skydive.

Murray worked for Bank of America and jumped as a hobby on her days off.

At about 4,500 feet, her main parachute failed to open.

She pulled her reserve. It opened at around 700 feet and then collapsed almost immediately.

That collapsed canopy still held back some of her speed. A person in true free fall hits the ground at about 120 miles an hour. Murray hit at about 80.

What she landed on was a fire ant mound, a pile of loose soil softer than the packed ground around it.

The impact still shattered the entire right side of her body and knocked the fillings out of her teeth. She spent two weeks in a coma at Carolinas Medical Center and went through 20 reconstructive surgeries and 17 blood transfusions.

The medical team counted more than 200 fire ant stings on her body. Her doctors put forward a theory that the shock from all that venom flooded her system with adrenaline and kept her heart beating while she waited for help. The theory has never been proven.

Two years later, she went skydiving again.

## 8. Two Brothers on a Platform

On December 7th, 2007, Alcides Moreno, thirty seven years old, stepped onto a suspended work platform on the 47th floor of the Solow Tower on New York's Upper East Side with his younger brother Edgar, to start the morning window cleaning shift.

The two brothers washed windows on high rise buildings. It was an ordinary workday.

The cables holding the platform slipped off their anchor point on the roof.

The whole platform dropped straight down with both men on it, 472 feet. Edgar Moreno came off the platform and died the instant he hit the ground.

Alcides Moreno never left the platform.

High rise window washers are trained for exactly this moment. Get flat on the deck and hold on. Moreno did that, and he rode the whole platform down.

The platform became a large flat surface pushing against the air the entire way. It also fell into a narrow alley between two buildings instead of open ground, so air compressed underneath it and its edges scraped the walls on both sides. When it hit, the aluminum deck crumpled and absorbed the force the way a car's crumple zone does, and it came down on a pile of cables.

A fall of just three stories kills about half the people who take one. People who fall more than ten stories almost never survive. Moreno fell forty seven and lived.

He broke both legs, an arm, a foot, and multiple ribs. He crushed a vertebra, collapsed both lungs, swelled his brain, and ruptured several organs. Doctors put 24 units of blood and 19 units of plasma into him before they could stop the bleeding. He went through 15 more surgeries and spent weeks in a coma. His vertebra was shattered, but his spinal cord was never cut.

On Christmas Day of 2007, Moreno opened his mouth and spoke his first words since the fall.

The hospital's chief of surgery said of the case that if you believe in miracles, this is one.

## 9. The Girl Who Walked Out of the Amazon

On December 24th, 1971, Juliane Koepcke, seventeen years old, boarded LANSA Flight 508 from Lima to Pucallpa with her mother, on her way to spend Christmas with her father.

Both of Koepcke's parents were German zoologists. Three years earlier they had taken her out of Lima and into the Amazon to build the Panguana research station, and that is where she learned how to live in the rainforest. Her father had warned the two of them not to fly LANSA because the airline had a bad safety reputation, but every other flight was full. Juliane had wanted to attend her high school graduation on December 23rd, so they pushed their trip home to the 24th.

Partway through the flight, the plane flew into a thunderstorm and was struck by lightning.

The aircraft broke apart in the air at about 10,000 feet.

Koepcke fell into the Amazon still strapped into her entire row of three seats. Experts later concluded that the row of seats spun like a helicopter rotor and slowed her descent, helped by the updraft inside the storm and the thick forest canopy underneath.

She woke up on the ground with a broken collarbone, a torn ligament in her left knee, a deep gash on her right arm, an eye injury, and a concussion. Out of 92 people on that plane, she was the only one who walked away from the crash site.

Her father had taught her one rule. If you get lost in the forest, find running water and follow it downstream, because water leads to a river, and rivers lead to people.

She found a creek and followed it for eleven days. The wound on her arm filled with maggots. On the ninth day she found a hut belonging to lumber workers. A few hours later the workers came back, poured gasoline into her wound to kill the maggots, and took her by canoe on an eleven hour trip to an inhabited area.

Investigators later determined that as many as 14 other passengers had also survived the initial crash, and died waiting to be found.

Koepcke recovered, then went back into the jungle with search parties to help locate the bodies. Her mother's body was found on January 12th, 1972.

She studied biology, earned a doctorate, and became an expert on bats. In 2000, after her father died, she took over the Panguana research station, in the middle of the forest that had nearly killed her.
