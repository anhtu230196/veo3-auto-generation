## Story 1: Elsie and Frances – The Fairies at the Bottom of the Garden

In the summer of 1917, in Cottingley, a small village just outside the city of Bradford in the north of England, two cousins kept getting into trouble for the very same thing. Elsie Wright was 16. Her cousin Frances Griffiths was only 9. Frances and her mother had recently come back to England from South Africa, and they were staying with Elsie's family. At the bottom of the Wrights' garden ran a little stream, what the locals call a beck. The girls spent hours down there, and every time they came home with soaking wet shoes and muddy clothes.

When the grown-ups asked what they could possibly be doing down there to get so wet, the girls had an answer ready. They were going to see the fairies.

Nobody believed them. So one day in July 1917, Elsie borrowed her father's camera. Arthur Wright was a keen amateur photographer, with his own darkroom right there in the house. The girls took the camera down to the stream, and about half an hour later they came back looking triumphant.

That evening, Arthur developed the glass plate himself. As the image slowly appeared in the darkroom, he saw Frances sitting behind a bush, her chin resting on her hand, looking straight into the camera. Right in front of her face, 4 tiny winged figures were dancing.

Arthur didn't believe it for a second. He knew his daughter was a gifted artist, and Elsie had spent some time working in a photographer's studio. He said the figures were nothing but cardboard cutouts and called the whole thing a prank. About 2 months later, the girls borrowed the camera again and came back with a second picture. This one showed Elsie sitting on the lawn, holding out her hand toward a gnome about one foot tall. After that, Arthur refused to lend them the camera at all.

His wife Polly, though, believed the photographs were real.

Near the end of 1918, Frances wrote to an old friend back in Cape Town. It was funny, she wrote, that she had never seen the fairies in Africa. It must have been too hot for them there.

In 1919, Polly went to a lecture given by the Theosophical Society in Bradford, a group of people deeply interested in the spirit world. The topic that evening was the life of fairies. When the talk was over, Polly showed the speaker the 2 photographs. They were passed around the society until they reached Edward Gardner, one of its leading members. Gardner became convinced that for the very first time in history, fairies had appeared clearly enough to be captured on a photographic plate.

Gardner took the original plates to a photography expert named Harold Snelling. After studying them closely, Snelling declared that both negatives were genuine, with no sign of studio trickery using paper or card. These were straightforward photographs, he said, of whatever had been in front of the camera at the time. The Kodak company examined them too. Kodak couldn't find any sign of faking either, but it refused to certify that they were real pictures of fairies.

The next person to hear about the photographs was Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, the creator of Sherlock Holmes, the most famous detective in all of fiction, a man who trusted nothing but observation and logic. In real life, Doyle was a committed spiritualist. The Strand Magazine had just asked him to write an article about fairies for its Christmas issue of 1920, and the Cottingley photographs reached him at exactly the right moment.

In the summer of 1920, Gardner traveled to Cottingley himself. He brought 2 new cameras and 24 photographic plates that had been secretly marked, so that nobody could swap them. Frances had moved away by then, but she was invited back to stay for the school holidays. The girls explained that the fairies wouldn't come out if anyone was watching. It rained for days on end, and the weather didn't clear until August 19th. That day, Polly went off to visit one of her sisters, leaving the 2 girls at home with the cameras.

That day, and again 2 days later, they took 3 more photographs. In one, a fairy leaps up right beside Frances's nose. In another, a fairy stands on a branch, offering Elsie a little bunch of harebells. And the fifth photograph has no girls in it at all. Just pale, misty fairy shapes among the grass. It became known as "Fairies and Their Sun-Bath."

Doyle was away on a lecture tour in Australia at the time. When he heard the news, he sent a telegram from Melbourne saying his heart was gladdened by 3 wonderful pictures that confirmed everything they had published.

In December 1920, Doyle's article, "Fairies Photographed," appeared in the Christmas issue of The Strand, along with the 2 photographs from 1917. The issue sold out within days. The girls' real names were kept out of print, and they appeared as "Alice" and "Iris." Doyle wrote that once people accepted that fairies were real, it would jolt the heavy, materialistic mind of the 20th century out of its ruts. In 1922, he published a whole book on the subject, called The Coming of the Fairies.

The press was split. One paper remarked dryly that what this story needed wasn't an understanding of the occult but an understanding of children. But Doyle didn't back down.

In the summer of 1921, Gardner came back one more time, bringing a clairvoyant with him. The clairvoyant said he could see fairies everywhere. The girls saw nothing, and not a single new photograph was taken. Years later, the cousins admitted they had simply played along with him for fun.

After that, the story slowly faded. The girls grew up, got married, and spent years living abroad. But journalists never quite forgot them. In 1966, a reporter tracked Elsie down. She said the fairies might have been figments of her imagination, but she left open the possibility that she had somehow photographed her own thoughts. In 1976, on television, both women agreed that a rational person doesn't see fairies. But they still wouldn't admit to faking anything. Meanwhile, skeptics were enlarging the photographs with computers, and a photography expert went back over them from the very beginning. They all came to the same conclusion. The pictures were fake.

In 1983, in a magazine called The Unexplained, the 2 elderly cousins finally admitted it.

Elsie had drawn the fairies herself. She copied the figures of dancing girls from a popular children's book of the time, Princess Mary's Gift Book, changed a few details, and added wings. The cousins cut the figures out, stood them up in the grass with hatpins, took their pictures, and then threw the props into the stream. Arthur Wright had guessed right on that very first night in his darkroom. And Harold Snelling hadn't been wrong either. The plates had never been tampered with. They showed exactly what had been in front of the camera. It just happened to be made of paper.

Elsie explained their long silence simply. They were just 2 village kids, she said, and up against a brilliant man like Conan Doyle, all they could do was keep quiet. Frances, in a letter written in 1983, said she had hated those photographs since she was 16 years old.

But there was one photograph the cousins never agreed on. Elsie said all 5 were fakes. Frances insisted, for the rest of her life, that the fifth one, the sun bath, was real. She said it was a wet Saturday afternoon, Elsie had nothing prepared, and Frances saw the fairies slowly taking shape in the grass, so she simply raised the camera and took the picture. One photography expert suggested it might have been an accidental double exposure, made while some of the paper fairies were still lying in the grass. If so, both women could have been telling the truth as they saw it.

Frances died in 1986. Elsie died in 1988. Neither of them ever changed her story about that last photograph.

## Story 2: Charles Dawson – The Jaw in the Piltdown Gravel Pit

Charles Dawson was a lawyer in the small town of Uckfield, in Sussex, in the south of England. In his spare time, he was a well-known amateur collector of antiquities. He found Roman figurines, Stone Age hand axes, and ancient wooden boats, so many remarkable things that the newspapers nicknamed him "the Wizard of Sussex."

According to Dawson, sometime around 1908, a workman digging gravel on the grounds of Barkham Manor, near the village of Piltdown, handed him a piece of a skull. Dawson said the workmen had smashed an entire skull, thinking it was a fossilized coconut. Over the following years, he picked up a few more fragments.

In February 1912, Dawson wrote to Arthur Smith Woodward, the Keeper of Geology at the Natural History Museum in London. That summer, the 2 men went digging in the gravel pit, along with a young French Jesuit priest. They found more pieces of skull, teeth, stone tools, and the bones of ancient animals. The most important pieces, more of the skull and half of a lower jaw, were all found by Dawson alone.

It was the jaw that stunned the scientific world. At first glance, it looked almost exactly like the jaw of a young chimpanzee. But its 2 molars were worn flat, the way human teeth wear down from chewing.

On December 18th, 1912, at a meeting of the Geological Society of London, Woodward announced a new species of early human. He gave it the scientific name Eoanthropus dawsoni, meaning "Dawson's dawn man." His reconstruction showed a skull much like a modern human's, with a brain about two-thirds the size of ours, attached to the jaw of an ape. It fit perfectly with what many experts in England believed at the time, that as our ancestors became human, the brain grew large first and everything else followed. The newspapers called it the missing link between apes and humans.

In 1913, the young priest found a canine tooth in the same gravel pit, and Woodward said it fit the jaw perfectly. That same year, an anatomy professor at King's College London argued in the journal Nature that this was simply an ape's jaw lying next to a human skull. A few scientists in France and America thought the same. But their voices were drowned out.

In 1915, Dawson reported finding a second skull, which became known as Piltdown 2, at another site about 2 miles away. The exact location was never recorded. For many people who were still on the fence, 2 separate finds at 2 separate sites made Piltdown much harder to dismiss.

In August 1916, Charles Dawson died. From that day on, the Piltdown gravel pit never produced another piece of bone.

For decades afterward, Piltdown Man appeared in textbooks, in museums, and in more than 250 scientific papers. In 1924, an anatomist in South Africa announced the fossil of a young ancient ape-human with a small skull but a very humanlike jaw and teeth, the exact opposite of Piltdown. His discovery was brushed aside for years, partly because it didn't fit with Piltdown.

In the late 1940s, Kenneth Oakley, a geologist at the Natural History Museum, tried out a new technique. He measured how much fluorine the bones had absorbed from the soil over time. The results showed that the Piltdown bones were far younger than the age everyone had given them.

In 1953, Joseph Weiner, an anthropologist at Oxford University, began talking over the Piltdown puzzle with Oakley. Weiner went back through the reports and the casts of the fossils, and he noticed that the 2 molars were worn down just a little too evenly, as if someone had ground them with something.

His team took some chimpanzee teeth, filed them down, and stained them. The result looked almost identical to Piltdown. Then they put the real Piltdown teeth under a microscope, and the file marks were plain to see. X-rays showed that no new dentin had grown in beneath the worn surface, which meant the wear hadn't happened while the owner of those teeth was alive.

In November 1953, the findings were made public. Piltdown Man was a patchwork. The skull came from a human who had lived in the Middle Ages. The jaw came from an orangutan that had died about 500 years earlier. There were also a few fossil chimpanzee teeth. Everything had been stained with an iron solution and chromic acid to make it look ancient. And the 2 flat, humanlike molars that had turned an ape's jaw into the missing link had been filed down by hand.

For decades, the list of suspects kept growing. Then in 2016, after 8 years of research, a team from the Natural History Museum and Liverpool John Moores University published their conclusion. There had been only one forger. The methods used on Piltdown 1 and Piltdown 2 were the same, from the way the bones were stained, to the gravel packed into their cracks, to the dental putty used to fix the teeth in place. DNA analysis even showed that the teeth from both sites came from the same orangutan. The team concluded that the forger was Charles Dawson. Another archaeologist went back through Dawson's lifelong collection and counted at least 38 fakes.

The one person who never found out was Arthur Smith Woodward. When he retired in 1924, he bought a house near Piltdown, and for the next 20 years he kept digging in that same gravel pit, the one that hadn't given up a single bone since the day Dawson died. Woodward passed away in 1944, 9 years before Piltdown Man was exposed. He never found anything else.

## Story 3: Ferdinand Demara – The Surgeon of the Cayuga

In 1951, off the west coast of Korea, the Canadian destroyer HMCS Cayuga took on board 3 South Korean guerrilla fighters who had been badly wounded in a raid. There was only one doctor on the ship, Surgeon Lieutenant Joseph Cyr. He ordered the men carried into the operating room and prepared for surgery. Then, as he himself would tell it years later, he went straight to his cabin, opened a textbook on general surgery, and speed-read the operations he was about to perform.

He had never studied medicine. He wasn't a doctor. And his name wasn't Joseph Cyr.

Ferdinand Waldo Demara was born in December 1921 in Lawrence, Massachusetts. His family was comfortable until the Great Depression wiped them out. At 16, he ran away from home and joined a Trappist monastery in Rhode Island.

From then on, he lived his life as one person after another. He borrowed an army buddy's name to get into a monastery in Kentucky. He became Dr. Robert Linton French, a psychologist, teaching at a Catholic college in Pennsylvania and rising to dean of its philosophy school. He turned up in Maine as a religious brother named John Payne, and helped found a college in the town of Alfred.

In Maine, he got to know a young Canadian doctor named Joseph Cyr, who practiced medicine in the town of Grand Falls, New Brunswick. By the time Demara left Maine, he had Cyr's medical credentials in his hands.

In March 1951, at a recruiting office in Saint John, New Brunswick, "Dr. Joseph Cyr" volunteered for the Royal Canadian Navy, just as the Korean War was creating an urgent need for medical officers. A process that normally took months was done in a matter of days. He was commissioned as a surgeon lieutenant, spent some time at the naval hospital in Halifax, and was then posted to the Cayuga at Esquimalt, as the ship got ready for her second tour in Korean waters.

His first real test on board was the captain's own tooth. Captain James Plomer had an inflamed molar, and "Dr. Cyr" pulled it. Years later, Demara admitted he "didn't know a lot of dentistry."

For weeks after that, he treated the crew's cuts, sprains, broken bones, and minor infections. And then came the day the 3 wounded fighters were brought on board.

With the textbook read, he walked into the operating room. He removed a bullet lodged in one man's chest. He amputated another man's foot. One officer on board later remembered that when the guerrillas came under fire from the shore, Demara moved calmly among the wounded, dressing their injuries as if he didn't have a care in the world.

A navy doctor pulling bullets out of wounded men at sea, in the middle of a war, was exactly the kind of story newspapers loved. News of the heroic "Dr. Joseph Cyr" went out over the wire services and into papers all across Canada.

One of the people who read it was the mother of Dr. Joseph Cyr. Her son, on the very days he was supposedly operating off the coast of Korea, was still seeing patients back home in Grand Falls, New Brunswick.

Naval headquarters sent a signal to the Cayuga, for the captain's eyes only. They had reason to believe the ship's medical officer was an impostor.

When he was confronted, Demara locked himself in his cabin for 3 days before he finally came up on deck. He told his shipmates he had just hoped the ship would make it back to Esquimalt, and then he would have quietly slipped away.

The Canadian navy never pressed charges. He was discharged quietly and honorably, and he even left with close to 1,000 dollars in back pay and allowances. He was driven to the American border at Blaine, Washington, and handed over to US immigration.

In January 1952, he sold his story to Life magazine. That same article tripped him up on his very next impersonation. Using forged papers, he landed a job at a prison in Huntsville, Texas, until an inmate came across the issue of Life with his photo in it. In 1960, a book about him called The Great Impostor became a bestseller, and the following year it was made into a movie, with Tony Curtis playing Demara. Later in life, he worked as a chaplain at a hospital in Anaheim, California. When his past came to light there, the hospital's chief of staff personally vouched for him. Ferdinand Demara died in June 1982, at the age of 60.

About 25 years after that voyage in 1951, the crew of the Cayuga held a reunion. Demara was there too. This time, he came as the Reverend Joseph Ferdinand Demara.

## Story 4: Gerd Heidemann – The Black Notebooks from the Crashed Plane

In late April 1945, with the Soviet army closing in on Berlin, a Junkers Ju 352 transport plane took off from the city as part of an operation to fly Hitler's personal belongings south. On board were 10 heavy chests, guarded by Hitler's personal valet, Wilhelm Arndt. The plane never arrived. It came down in the Heidenholz forest near the village of Börnersdorf, not far from the Czechoslovak border. According to later accounts, when Hitler heard the news, he said he had trusted that valet with extremely valuable documents that would show future generations the truth about what he had done.

That plane crash really happened. Some 35 years later, it became the foundation of one of the biggest hoaxes in the history of journalism.

Gerd Heidemann was a veteran reporter and photographer for Stern, one of West Germany's biggest news magazines. He had covered wars all over Africa and the Middle East, and his colleagues called him "the Bloodhound." In 1973, he went to photograph a yacht called the Carin II, which had once belonged to Hermann Göring, and ended up mortgaging his own apartment to buy it. The yacht was expensive to keep, and he borrowed more and more money from his own employer. It also drew him into the world of collectors of Nazi memorabilia.

At the end of 1979, in that world, Heidemann heard that a collector in Stuttgart owned one of Hitler's diaries. In January 1980, he got to see it with his own eyes. The owner said it had come from a plane crash in East Germany, but he refused to say who his supplier was.

Heidemann followed the trail. In November 1980, together with Stern's head of contemporary history, he traveled to the area around Dresden, behind the Iron Curtain, and found the graves of the crew of that Ju 352. The plane was real, the crash was real, and the graves were real.

In January 1981, Heidemann finally got hold of the supplier's phone number. He was a dealer in old military goods in Stuttgart who called himself Mr. Fischer. Mr. Fischer said there were as many as 27 diaries, being smuggled out of East Germany a few at a time. The management of Stern's parent company approved a 200,000 mark deposit after one short meeting, without consulting a single historian, and without going through the magazine's normal editorial channels.

On February 17th, 1981, the first 3 volumes reached Heidemann. They were black notebooks with a black ribbon held by a wax seal, and on each cover were 2 gold letters in an old Gothic script.

By June 1982, Stern had 35 volumes. The price kept climbing, at one point reaching 200,000 marks for a single diary. In the end, Stern paid a total of 9,300,000 West German marks, roughly 3,700,000 dollars at the time.

In April 1982, Stern sent some of the pages off for handwriting analysis, along with samples of Hitler's handwriting to compare them against. The pages went to 2 experts from the German Federal Archives, a former head of forensics for the Zurich police, and an American forensic document examiner. Every one of them concluded that the handwriting in the diaries and the handwriting in the samples came from the same person. The American expert said there was just no question about it.

In early 1983, Stern began offering the publishing rights to major outlets, including Newsweek, Paris Match, and the newspaper group of Rupert Murdoch, owner of The Times and The Sunday Times in Britain. The Times asked the historian Hugh Trevor-Roper to examine the diaries. He was one of the world's leading experts on Hitler, the man who had investigated Hitler's death for British intelligence right after the war.

Trevor-Roper was taken to the Swiss bank vault where Stern kept the diaries. He was told, falsely, that the paper had been chemically tested and shown to date from before the war. But what convinced him most was the sheer number of volumes. He reasoned that nobody would forge 60 volumes when 6 would have been enough.

On April 22nd, 1983, Stern announced its discovery to the world. The next day, The Times ran Trevor-Roper's article, in which he wrote that he was now satisfied the documents were authentic.

But that same evening, Trevor-Roper changed his mind. He told the paper he was having serious doubts, just as the presses were rolling on the next edition of The Sunday Times, the one carrying the diary story. Murdoch was told, and he decided not to stop the presses.

On April 25th, at a big press conference in Hamburg, Trevor-Roper told the assembled reporters that German experts needed to examine the diaries further, and he said he regretted that normal methods of historical verification had been sacrificed for a journalistic scoop. Near the end, a man in the audience stood up and demanded to know whether anyone had tested the ink, and security guards hauled him out in the middle of a scuffle.

Just 3 days later, Stern went ahead anyway with a special issue featuring excerpts from the diaries.

On May 4th, 1983, Stern handed 3 complete volumes to the German Federal Archives for a full forensic examination. The results came back only 2 days later. Under ultraviolet light, the paper glowed, a sign of an ingredient that simply shouldn't have been in paper from that era. The bindings contained polyester threads, which weren't manufactured before 1953. The ink was modern, and tests showed the writing had been done within the past 2 years. The content was full of historical mistakes, some of them copied word for word from a collection of Hitler's speeches and proclamations published after the war.

The West German government announced the results, 5 minutes before Stern could admit it on its own.

Mr. Fischer's real name was Konrad Kujau. He was born in East Germany in 1938, fled to West Germany in 1957, lived for years under false names, and by the mid-1970s was running a shop in Stuttgart selling Nazi memorabilia. Much of what he sold, he had made himself. Paintings "by Hitler," poems "by Hitler," and handwritten notes "by" other Nazi leaders. When Kujau saw his own name in the news and learned that Stern had paid more than 9 million marks, he took his wife and his mistress to Austria, then called a lawyer and turned himself in. When police searched his home, they found blank notebooks identical to the ones used for the diaries.

According to Kujau, each diary took him about 3 hours to write. He took most of the content from that same book of speeches, thinned his ink with water, sprinkled tea over the pages, and banged the notebooks against his desk to make them look old. And the samples of Hitler's "genuine" handwriting that the experts had used for comparison back in 1982 had partly come from items circulating among collectors. Kujau had written those too. The experts had been comparing one forgery against another.

On May 26th, 1983, Kujau wrote out a full confession, in which he said Heidemann had known all along that the diaries were fake. Heidemann was arrested that very evening. The trial in Hamburg ran from August 1984 to July 1985. Heidemann was sentenced to 4 years and 8 months in prison, and Kujau to 4 years and 6 months. The court found that Heidemann had pocketed 1,700,000 marks of Stern's money. At least 5 million marks were never accounted for. The judge said it was Stern's own carelessness that had persuaded him to go easier on the 2 men. Stern's 2 editors-in-chief lost their jobs, and it took the magazine about 10 years to win back its reputation. After prison, Kujau opened a gallery in Stuttgart, selling copies of paintings by Dalí and Miró, signed with his own name.

As for the 2 gold Gothic letters on the covers, the ones that so many people at Stern had held in their hands for 2 whole years, they were not A and H, for Adolf Hitler. Kujau had used a ready-made set of Gothic letters, and he had picked the F by mistake instead of the A. The covers of the Hitler Diaries read F.H.

## Story 5: Anna Anderson – The Nameless Woman of Berlin

On February 17th, 1920, in Berlin, a police sergeant pulled a young woman out of the Landwehr Canal after she jumped from the Bendler Bridge. She carried no papers of any kind. When asked her name, she wouldn't answer. When asked where she came from, she said nothing.

She was taken to a hospital and then moved to the Dalldorf asylum on the outskirts of Berlin, where she was entered in the records as Fräulein Unbekannt, which means "Miss Unknown." Doctors noted scars on her head and on her body. Staff at the asylum said she spoke German with what sounded like a Russian accent. She stayed there for almost 2 years, saying almost nothing about herself.

In early 1922, another patient declared that the nameless woman was Grand Duchess Tatiana, a daughter of Tsar Nicholas II of Russia. In July 1918, Bolshevik forces had shot the Tsar, the Empress, their 4 daughters, and their son in the city of Yekaterinburg, and hidden the bodies. No grave was ever found. Among Russian exiles, the rumor that one of the children had survived never quite died.

The other patient was released and told her story to an exiled Russian officer, who came to visit and believed it. Then a former lady-in-waiting to the Empress, Baroness Buxhoeveden, came to see for herself. She looked at the woman and said she was too short to be Tatiana. A few days later, the nameless woman spoke up. "I never said I was Tatiana."

From then on, the name people spoke of was Anastasia, the Tsar's youngest daughter. The woman began calling herself Anna Tschaikovsky, with "Anna" as a short form of Anastasia. Her supporters believed the scars on her head and body were bayonet wounds from the night of the killings in Yekaterinburg.

In 1925, people who had once been close to Anastasia came to meet her, one after another. The tutor who had taught the Tsar's children French. Anastasia's former nursemaid. A former servant of the Empress. And even Grand Duchess Olga, Anastasia's own aunt. Every one of them showed her real sympathy. And in the end, every one of them said she was not Anastasia.

In 1927, the Grand Duke of Hesse, the late Empress's brother, hired a private detective to investigate. The trail led to a name. Franziska Schanzkowska, a young Polish woman from a farming family. During the First World War, Franziska worked in a factory making weapons. In 1916, not long after her fiancé was killed at the front, a grenade slipped from her hand and exploded. The foreman was killed right in front of her, and she was injured in the head. That September, she was declared insane, and she spent time in 2 different asylums. In early 1920, the people she lodged with in Berlin reported her missing, only a short time before the nameless woman was pulled out of the Landwehr Canal. If the investigation was right, the scars the Dalldorf doctors had recorded on her head never came from a basement in Yekaterinburg at all. They came from a grenade factory.

In May 1927, Franziska's brother Felix was brought to meet her at an inn. His sworn statement spoke only of "a strong resemblance," listed several differences, and said she didn't recognize him. Years later, Felix's family said he had known perfectly well it was his sister, but chose to leave her to her new and far more comfortable life.

In 1928, Gleb Botkin, the son of the royal family's personal doctor, who had been killed alongside them, brought her to America. In New York, to avoid the press, she was checked into a hotel under the name Mrs. Anderson. The name stayed with her for the rest of her life. That same year, the 12 closest surviving Romanov relatives signed a joint statement dismissing her story as nothing more than a fairy tale.

In 1931, she went back to Germany. In 1938, the Nazi authorities arranged for her to meet the Schanzkowski family. Gertrude, one of Franziska's sisters, insisted that the woman in front of her was Franziska. But the family refused to sign any statement against her.

Also in 1938, her lawyers went to court in Germany to have her recognized and to claim the Tsar's inheritance. The case dragged on until 1970, the longest-running lawsuit in German history. The final ruling said she had failed to prove she was Anastasia, but that her identity was "neither established nor refuted."

In 1968, she moved to America once more, to Charlottesville, Virginia, where Gleb Botkin was living. On December 23rd, 1968, just days before her visa ran out, she married Jack Manahan, a history professor 20 years younger than her. Botkin was the best man. Manahan liked to call himself "son-in-law to the Tsar."

In August 1979, she was taken to Martha Jefferson Hospital in Charlottesville with a blocked intestine. Surgeons removed a section of her bowel. The hospital kept a small sample of tissue from the operation, sealed in paraffin wax.

On February 12th, 1984, she died of pneumonia. Her body was cremated that same day. Her ashes were buried in the churchyard at Seeon Castle, in Bavaria, Germany.

In 1991, the remains of the Tsar, the Empress, and 3 of their daughters were excavated near Yekaterinburg and identified through DNA. One of the samples used for comparison came from Prince Philip of Britain, whose maternal grandmother was the Russian Empress's sister.

The woman who claimed to be Anastasia had been cremated. But the tissue sample at Martha Jefferson Hospital was still there. In 1994, a forensic team in Britain, along with a second team working at the same time, analyzed the mitochondrial DNA in that sample. It didn't match the Romanov remains, and it didn't match any living Romanov relatives. But it did match Karl Maucher, a grandson of Gertrude, the sister who had insisted back in 1938 that this woman was Franziska. A lock of hair found in an envelope tucked inside one of Manahan's books gave the same result.

In 2007, the remains of the last 2 Romanov children, the son and one of the daughters, were found nearby. DNA tests later confirmed that all 7 members of the Tsar's family had now been found. None of them had escaped Yekaterinburg.

Her gravestone at Seeon reads "Anastasia Manahan," with the name Anastasia also written in Russian letters, and the birth year 1901, the year the princess was born. Franziska Schanzkowska was born in 1896.

## Story 6: Han van Meegeren – The Vermeer in the Salt Mine

On May 17th, 1945, just over a week after the war in Europe ended, Allied soldiers were taking stock of the Altaussee salt mine, high in the mountains of Austria. Deep inside, the Nazis had hidden thousands of works of art looted from all over Europe. Among them was the private collection of Hermann Göring, the second most powerful man in Nazi Germany. And inside Göring's collection, an Allied officer found a painting no one had ever heard of. It was called Christ and the Adulteress, and it bore the name of Johannes Vermeer.

Vermeer was a Dutch master of the 17th century, the painter of Girl with a Pearl Earring. Very few of his paintings survive, and every one of them is a national treasure in the Netherlands. Göring had given up 137 paintings in exchange for this one.

The sales records led back to a German banker and art dealer, and from him to the man who had sold the painting in the first place. He was Han van Meegeren, a painter in Amsterdam. On May 29th, 1945, Van Meegeren was arrested for collaborating with the enemy, for selling a piece of Dutch cultural heritage to the Nazis. It was a crime that could carry the death penalty.

After a few weeks in custody, Van Meegeren said something nobody expected. The painting in Göring's hands wasn't a Vermeer. It was a Van Meegeren. He had painted it himself.

Han van Meegeren was born in 1889 in Deventer, in the Netherlands. He studied architecture, then art, and in 1913 he won a gold medal for a painting of a church interior. By the 1920s he was a well-known painter working in a traditional style. But the critics grew colder and colder toward him. One critic wrote that he was a gifted technician who had every virtue except originality.

Van Meegeren later said he turned to forgery to prove to the critics that he could do more than just copy the old masters. One of his biographers believed the real motive was money, to pay for a lavish lifestyle and his addictions.

In 1932, he moved to a villa in the south of France and spent 6 years working on one problem. He wanted a painting he had only just finished to pass every test the experts could throw at it. He bought genuine 17th century paintings of little value and scraped off the paint so he could reuse the old canvases. He ground his own pigments from the same raw materials painters of that era had used, like lapis lazuli. He painted with badger-hair brushes. And he mixed a new synthetic resin called Bakelite into his paint, then baked the finished canvas at 212 to 248 degrees Fahrenheit. The paint hardened until it could resist alcohol and solvents, just like paint that had been drying for 3 centuries. He rolled the canvas around a cylinder to make the surface crack, then brushed India ink into the cracks so they looked like centuries of dust.

In 1937, Van Meegeren finished The Supper at Emmaus, showing Christ after the resurrection, sitting at supper with 2 of his disciples. Through a lawyer, the painting was brought to Abraham Bredius, the most celebrated Dutch authority on the painters of the Golden Age, who was 82 years old at the time. In September 1937, writing in The Burlington Magazine, Bredius called it the masterpiece of Johannes Vermeer.

The Rembrandt Society, with the help of a wealthy shipowner, bought the painting for 520,000 guilders and donated it to the Boijmans Museum in Rotterdam. In 1938, at a major exhibition celebrating Queen Wilhelmina's 40 years on the throne, with 450 works by the Dutch masters on display, critics called The Supper at Emmaus the spiritual center of the entire show.

During the war years, Van Meegeren kept painting and kept selling. More "Vermeers," and more paintings "by" Pieter de Hooch as well. He made somewhere between 5,500,000 and 7,500,000 guilders and bought dozens of houses. One of the last of these paintings was Christ and the Adulteress, sold to that German banker, and from there it went to Göring.

Nobody believed Van Meegeren's confession. So he made them an offer. He would paint a new "Vermeer" right in front of them. The authorities rented a house in Amsterdam, and starting in July 1945, under guard, in front of witnesses and reporters, he painted Jesus Among the Doctors in the style of Vermeer.

But a painting made in front of witnesses still wasn't enough for a court. An international commission, led by the Belgian chemist Paul Coremans, analyzed 8 of the suspect paintings. In the paint, they found Bakelite and another resin called Albertol, synthetic materials that only came into existence in the 20th century. In Van Meegeren's studio, there was a bottle of Albertol. The cracks were far too regular, and the India ink settled inside them looked nothing like real dust.

According to one account from the time, when Göring was told that his Vermeer was a fake, he looked as if he had discovered for the first time that there was evil in the world.

The trial opened in Amsterdam on October 29th, 1947. The charge of collaborating with the enemy had been replaced with forgery and fraud. To the Dutch public, Van Meegeren was now the man who had fooled Göring. A poll that October ranked him the second most popular person in the country, behind only the prime minister. On November 12th, 1947, the court found him guilty and sentenced him to one year in prison.

He never served a single day of that sentence. On November 26th, the last day he could appeal, he suffered a heart attack. On December 30th, 1947, he died in a hospital in Amsterdam, at the age of 58.

In 1967, scientists at Carnegie Mellon University in the United States measured the natural radioactivity in the white lead of The Supper at Emmaus and confirmed that the lead had been produced in the 20th century. The painting Bredius once called Vermeer's masterpiece still hangs in the Boijmans Museum in Rotterdam today, under the name of Han van Meegeren.

In 1950, Van Meegeren's belongings were auctioned off to pay his debts. Jesus Among the Doctors, the painting he had made under guard to save his own life, this time carrying the name of the man who actually painted it, sold for 3,000 guilders. It eventually made its way to South Africa, and today it hangs in a church in Johannesburg.
